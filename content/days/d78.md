## Theory

### Kinked policies

A policy variable $$B = b(X)$$ is a **continuous** function of the running variable with a **kink** at $$c$$: its slope changes from $$b'(c^-)$$ to $$b'(c^+)$$. Examples: unemployment benefits equal to 50% of past earnings up to a maximum; tax credits that phase in and out; co-payment schedules.

### The RKD estimand

If $$Y = g(B, X, \varepsilon)$$, then

$$
\tau_{RKD} = \frac{\lim_{x \downarrow c}\frac{d}{dx}E[Y \mid X = x] - \lim_{x \uparrow c}\frac{d}{dx}E[Y \mid X = x]}{b'(c^+) - b'(c^-)}
$$

identifies the (average) marginal effect of $$B$$ on $$Y$$ at $$c$$, under **smoothness**: the density of $$X$$ and the direct dependence of $$Y$$ on $$X$$ have continuous derivatives at $$c$$ (Card, Lee, Pei and Weber, 2015). With imperfect implementation (actual benefits deviate from the formula), a **fuzzy RKD** divides by the estimated kink in actual $$B$$.

### Estimation

Fit local polynomials on each side and compare the **slopes** at $$c$$ (local linear or local quadratic, with a kink term $$(X - c)\mathbf 1[X \ge c]$$). Derivatives are harder to estimate than levels: the variance of a slope estimate scales like $$1/(nh^3)$$ instead of $$1/(nh)$$, so RKD needs large samples (administrative data).

### Extensions of RD

- **Multiple cutoffs:** different cohorts or regions have different thresholds; normalise $$X - c_j$$ and pool (a weighted average of cutoff-specific effects), or exploit the variation to learn how effects change with the cutoff.
- **Geographic RD:** a boundary separates treated from untreated areas (school districts, states with different laws). The running variable is distance to the boundary (two-dimensional). Threats: other policies change at the same border; people sort across borders.
- **RD in time:** treatment starts on a date. Concerns: other things change at the same time, anticipation and adjustment, serial correlation; often closer to an interrupted time series than an RD.
- **Extrapolation:** with assumptions (for example, conditional independence of $$X$$ and outcomes given covariates), effects away from the cutoff can be estimated.

### Exercise 2 · An RKD estimate

Unemployment duration rises with earnings at 0.2 weeks per $100 of earnings below the kink and 0.1 weeks per $100 above. Estimate the effect of $100 more in weekly benefit on duration.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Slope change of duration: $$0.1 - 0.2 = -0.1$$ weeks per $100 of earnings.

**Step 2.** Slope change of benefits: $$-0.5$$ dollars per dollar, i.e. $$-50$$ dollars of benefit per $100 of earnings.

**Step 3.** $$\hat\tau = -0.1/(-50) = 0.002$$ weeks per dollar of weekly benefit, i.e. 0.2 weeks per $100 of benefit.

**Answer:** $100 more in weekly benefits lengthens unemployment by about 0.2 weeks.

</details>

### Exercise 3 · Why noisier?

Why are RKD estimates usually less precise than RD estimates from the same sample?

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** RD estimates a level; RKD estimates a derivative, which needs the spread of $$X$$ within the window to be large.

**Answer:** slope variance scales like $$1/(nh^3)$$; with the small bandwidths needed near the kink, it is large.

</details>

### Exercise 4 · Normalising cutoffs

Three states award a scholarship at test scores of 60, 65 and 70. How can the cutoffs be pooled?

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Define $$\tilde X = X - c_j$$ for a student in state $$j$$; all cutoffs are at $$\tilde X = 0$$.

**Step 2.** Estimate one RD on $$\tilde X$$, or separate RDs per state and average.

**Answer:** the pooled estimate is a weighted average of state-specific effects, weighted by the density of students near each cutoff.

</details>

### Exercise 5 · A school-district boundary

**Step 2.** The boundary may follow a road, river or neighbourhood divide that itself affects prices; sorting of households changes neighbourhood composition.

## Part 2 · Proofs

### Exercise 6 · RKD identification

Let $$Y = \beta B + m(X) + \varepsilon$$ with $$E[\varepsilon \mid X]$$ having a continuous derivative, $$m$$ differentiable, and $$B = b(X)$$ kinked at $$c$$. Prove $$\tau_{RKD} = \beta$$.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** $$E[Y \mid X = x] = \beta b(x) + m(x) + E[\varepsilon \mid X = x]$$.

**Step 2.** Derivative: $$\beta b'(x) + m'(x) + \frac{d}{dx}E[\varepsilon \mid X = x]$$.

**Step 3.** The last two terms are continuous at $$c$$; the slope change is $$\beta(b'(c^+) - b'(c^-))$$.

**Step 4.** Divide by the policy's slope change. $$\blacksquare$$

</details>

### Exercise 7 · Fuzzy RKD

If actual benefits $$B$$ deviate from $$b(X)$$ by noise unrelated to $$Y$$, show $$\tau$$ equals the outcome slope change divided by the slope change of $$E[B \mid X]$$.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** $$E[Y \mid X] = \beta E[B \mid X] + m(X) + E[\varepsilon \mid X]$$.

**Step 2.** Differentiate and take the change at $$c$$: $$\Delta\text{slope}_Y = \beta\,\Delta\text{slope}_{E[B \mid X]}$$.

**Step 3.** Divide. $$\blacksquare$$

</details>

### Exercise 8 · Variance of a slope estimate

With $$X - c$$ uniform on $$[0, h]$$, $$n_h \approx nf(c)h$$ observations and error variance $$\sigma^2$$, show that the variance of the OLS slope in a local linear fit is of order $$\sigma^2/(nf(c)h^3)$$.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** OLS slope variance: $$\sigma^2/(n_h\operatorname{Var}(X))$$.

**Step 2.** $$\operatorname{Var}(X) = h^2/12$$ for a uniform on $$[0, h]$$.

**Step 3.** $$\sigma^2/(nf(c)h \cdot h^2/12) = 12\sigma^2/(nf(c)h^3)$$. $$\blacksquare$$

</details>

### Exercise 9 · Pooling cutoffs

With cutoff-specific effects $$\tau_j$$ and $$n_j$$ observations near each cutoff, show that a pooled sharp RD on the normalised running variable estimates approximately $$\sum_j w_j\tau_j$$ with $$w_j \propto n_j$$ (when the regression functions have the same slopes).

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Near $$\tilde X = 0$$, the pooled right limit is the $$n_j$$-weighted average of the right limits, and likewise the left.

**Step 2.** Subtract: the pooled jump is $$\sum_j(n_j/\sum_k n_k)\tau_j$$. $$\blacksquare$$ If slopes differ by cutoff, cutoff-specific fits are needed.

</details>

### Exercise 10 · A coinciding kink

Suppose the tax schedule also has a kink at the same earnings level, so that $$m(X)$$ has a slope change $$\kappa$$ at $$c$$. Show the RKD estimate becomes $$\beta + \kappa/\Delta b'$$.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** The outcome slope change becomes $$\beta\Delta b' + \kappa$$.

**Step 2.** Divide by $$\Delta b'$$. $$\blacksquare$$ Any other policy kinked at the same point is a confounder for RKD, as a coinciding jump is for RD.

</details>

## Part 3 · Applied

### Exercise 11 · UI benefits and unemployment duration

Using administrative records on 100,000 claimants, an RKD estimate implies that $100 more in weekly benefits lengthens unemployment by 0.2 weeks (Exercise 2). At a typical benefit of $450 and duration of 18 weeks, compute the elasticity of duration with respect to benefits.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** A 1% rise in benefits is $4.50, lengthening duration by $$0.2 \times 4.5/100 = 0.009$$ weeks.

**Step 2.** As a share of 18 weeks: $$0.009/18 = 0.05\%$$.

**Step 3.** Elasticity: $$\varepsilon = (\Delta D/D)/(\Delta B/B) = (0.009/18)/0.01 = 0.05$$.

**Answer:** about 0.05, a small elasticity: at this margin, benefits barely lengthen unemployment.

</details>

### Exercise 12 · School quality and house prices

Across attendance-zone boundaries, houses on the side with schools scoring 5% higher on tests sell for 2.5% more, with houses within 0.2 miles of the boundary and boundary fixed effects. Interpret and give one robustness check.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Elasticity of house prices to school test scores ≈ $$2.5/5 = 0.5$$.

**Step 2.** Check: house and neighbourhood characteristics (size, age, demographics) should be balanced across the boundary; prices just outside the boundary in placebo directions should not jump.

**Answer:** parents value school quality capitalised at about half the percentage test-score gap.

</details>

### Exercise 13 · Pooling state scholarship cutoffs

Three states' scholarship RDs give effects on enrolment of 0.10, 0.06 and 0.04 with 2,000, 1,000 and 1,000 students near the cutoffs. Compute the pooled effect.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Weights 0.5, 0.25, 0.25.

**Step 2.** $$0.5(0.10) + 0.25(0.06) + 0.25(0.04) = 0.05 + 0.015 + 0.01 = 0.075$$.

**Answer:** 7.5 points; the spread across states suggests effects depend on the cutoff level or state context.

</details>

### Exercise 14 · RD in time: driving restrictions

A city bans cars with certain plate numbers from driving on given weekdays starting on 1 March. Hourly pollution readings around that date are used to estimate the effect. List three concerns.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Other changes at the same date (season, weather, other regulations).

**Step 2.** Adaptation: drivers buy second cars or shift trips over weeks, so short-run and long-run effects differ.

**Step 3.** Serial correlation in hourly pollution makes conventional standard errors far too small.

**Answer:** treat it as an interrupted time series: control for weather and season, check placebo dates, use autocorrelation-robust inference, and look at longer windows.

</details>

### Exercise 15 · An RKD in a simulation

In the simulation below, a benefit schedule with slope 0.5 is capped at $$X = 0$$; the true effect of benefits on the outcome is 0.8. Compare RKD estimates with local linear and local quadratic fits.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Local linear: mean about 0.43, badly biased, because the curvature of $$m(X)$$ within the bandwidth contaminates the slopes.

**Step 2.** Local quadratic: mean about 0.82, close to the truth, but with an SD near 1.2, four times larger.

**Answer:** RKD is feasible but fragile: slopes need flexible fits, and flexible fits need very large samples.

</details>

## Verify in Python

```python
import numpy as np

print("Ex 2:", -.1 / -50, "weeks per $ ->", -.1 / -50 * 100, "weeks per $100", " Ex 11 elasticity:", round((.2 * 4.5 / 100 / 18) / .01, 3),
      " Ex 13:", .5 * .10 + .25 * .06 + .25 * .04)

rng = np.random.default_rng(78)
def kink_slope(x, y, h, p):
    out = []
    for side in (x >= 0, x < 0):
        xx, yy = x[side], y[side]; w = np.clip(1 - np.abs(xx) / h, 0, None); m = w > 0
        X = np.column_stack([xx[m] ** k for k in range(p + 1)]); W = w[m]
        out.append(np.linalg.solve(X.T @ (W[:, None] * X), X.T @ (W * yy[m]))[1])
    return out[0] - out[1]
res = {1: [], 2: []}
for _ in range(300):
    x = rng.uniform(-1, 1, 50_000)
    B = .5 * np.minimum(x, 0) + 2                     # benefit: slope 0.5 below the cap, 0 above
    y = .8 * B + np.sin(1.5 * x) + .3 * x ** 2 + rng.normal(0, 1, x.size)
    for p in (1, 2):
        res[p].append(kink_slope(x, y, .4, p) / (0 - .5))
for p in (1, 2):
    print(f"local {'linear' if p == 1 else 'quadratic'}: mean {np.mean(res[p]):.3f}  sd {np.std(res[p]):.3f}  (truth 0.8)")
```

## Key takeaways

- RKD uses a change in a policy's slope: effect = (change in outcome slope) / (change in policy slope), under smoothness of everything else.
- Slopes are harder to estimate than levels (variance ∝ 1/(nh³)); RKD needs large data sets.
- Multiple cutoffs, geographic boundaries and RD in time extend the design, each with its own threats: coinciding policies, sorting, adaptation and serial correlation.
