## Theory

### Assumptions for large samples

Drop normality and homoskedasticity. Assume iid observations $$(y_i, x_i)$$ with

$$
y_i = x_i^{\top}\beta + \varepsilon_i, \qquad E[x_i\varepsilon_i] = 0, \qquad Q = E[x_ix_i^{\top}] \text{ invertible}.
$$

### Consistency

$$
\hat\beta - \beta = \Big(\tfrac1n\sum_ix_ix_i^{\top}\Big)^{-1}\Big(\tfrac1n\sum_ix_i\varepsilon_i\Big) \xrightarrow{p} Q^{-1}\cdot 0 = 0,
$$

by the LLN applied to each average and the **continuous mapping theorem** (continuous functions preserve convergence in probability).

### Asymptotic normality: the sandwich

By the CLT, $$\frac{1}{\sqrt n}\sum x_i\varepsilon_i \xrightarrow{d} N(0, \Omega)$$ with $$\Omega = E[\varepsilon_i^2x_ix_i^{\top}]$$. By **Slutsky's theorem**,

$$
\sqrt n(\hat\beta - \beta) \xrightarrow{d} N\big(0,\; Q^{-1}\Omega Q^{-1}\big).
$$

"Bread–meat–bread". Its sample analogue is the **heteroskedasticity-robust (HC0, White)** estimator

$$
\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\hat\beta) = (X^{\top}X)^{-1}\Big(\sum_ie_i^2x_ix_i^{\top}\Big)(X^{\top}X)^{-1},
$$

with HC1 multiplying by $$n/(n - k)$$ (Stata's `robust`). Under homoskedasticity, $$E[\varepsilon^2 \mid x] = \sigma^2$$, so $$\Omega = \sigma^2Q$$ and the sandwich collapses to $$\sigma^2Q^{-1}$$, the classical formula.

### Clustered data

If errors are correlated within clusters $$g = 1, \dots, G$$ but independent across them,

$$
\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}_{CR}(\hat\beta) = (X^{\top}X)^{-1}\Big(\sum_gX_g^{\top}e_ge_g^{\top}X_g\Big)(X^{\top}X)^{-1}.
$$

Its reliability depends on the number of **clusters** $$G$$, not the number of observations. With few clusters (say under 30–50), use small-sample corrections or the wild cluster bootstrap.

> **Causal link.** Treatment is often assigned at the cluster level (schools, villages, states). Inference must then be clustered at the level of assignment, or standard errors can be badly understated.

## Part 1 · Warm-up

### Exercise 1 · A scalar sandwich

In a no-intercept regression $$y = \beta x + \varepsilon$$, write the classical and the HC0 variance of $$\hat\beta$$.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Classical: $$s^2/\sum x_i^2$$.

**Step 2.** HC0: $$\sum x_i^2e_i^2/(\sum x_i^2)^2$$.

**Step 3.** The robust version weights each squared residual by its $$x_i^2$$.

</details>

### Exercise 2 · Numbers

Take $$x = (1, 2, 3)$$ and residuals $$e = (0.5, -1, 0.5)$$ (no intercept, $$k = 1$$). Compute both variances.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** $$\sum x^2 = 1 + 4 + 9 = 14$$.

**Step 2.** Classical: $$s^2 = (0.25 + 1 + 0.25)/(3 - 1) = 0.75$$, so $$\operatorname{Var} = 0.75/14 \approx 0.0536$$.

**Step 3.** HC0: $$\sum x^2e^2 = 1(0.25) + 4(1) + 9(0.25) = 6.5$$, so $$\operatorname{Var} = 6.5/14^2 = 6.5/196 \approx 0.0332$$.

**Answer:** 0.0536 (classical) versus 0.0332 (robust). (These residuals satisfy $$\sum xe = 0.5 - 2 + 1.5 = 0$$, as OLS residuals must.)

</details>

### Exercise 3 · HC1

Apply the HC1 correction to Exercise 2.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

$$n/(n - k) = 3/2$$: $$0.0332 \times 1.5 \approx 0.0497$$.

</details>

### Exercise 4 · When do they agree?

Under what condition do classical and robust SEs agree in large samples, and what does a large gap suggest?

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

Under homoskedasticity (more precisely, when $$\varepsilon^2$$ is uncorrelated with $$x_ix_i^{\top}$$). A large gap signals heteroskedasticity, or sometimes model misspecification.

</details>

### Exercise 5 · Counting clusters

A study has 10,000 pupils in 20 schools, with treatment assigned by school. How much information is there for inference on the treatment effect?

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

Effectively 20 clusters (10 treated, 10 control). Cluster-robust inference treats each school as one draw; with so few, use a $$t_{G-1}$$ reference distribution or the wild cluster bootstrap.

</details>

## Part 2 · Proofs

### Exercise 6 · Consistency

Prove $$\hat\beta \xrightarrow{p} \beta$$ under the assumptions above.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Substitute $$y_i = x_i^{\top}\beta + \varepsilon_i$$ into $$\hat\beta$$:

$$
\hat\beta - \beta = \Big(\tfrac1n\textstyle\sum_ix_ix_i^{\top}\Big)^{-1}\Big(\tfrac1n\sum_ix_i\varepsilon_i\Big).
$$

**Step 2.** LLN, applied entry by entry: $$\frac1n\sum x_ix_i^{\top} \xrightarrow{p} Q$$.

**Step 3.** LLN again: $$\frac1n\sum x_i\varepsilon_i \xrightarrow{p} E[x\varepsilon] = 0$$.

**Step 4.** Matrix inversion is continuous at the invertible $$Q$$, so by the continuous mapping theorem the product converges to $$Q^{-1}\cdot 0 = 0$$.

**Step 5.** Note what was *not* needed: normality, homoskedasticity, or even $$E[\varepsilon \mid x] = 0$$; only $$E[x\varepsilon] = 0$$. $$\blacksquare$$

</details>

### Exercise 7 · The sandwich

Derive $$\sqrt n(\hat\beta - \beta) \xrightarrow{d} N(0, Q^{-1}\Omega Q^{-1})$$.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Scale Step 1 of Exercise 6 by $$\sqrt n$$: $$\sqrt n(\hat\beta - \beta) = \hat Q^{-1}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt n}\sum_ix_i\varepsilon_i$$, with $$\hat Q = \frac1n\sum x_ix_i^{\top}$$.

**Step 2.** The vectors $$x_i\varepsilon_i$$ are iid with mean 0 and covariance $$\Omega = E[\varepsilon^2xx^{\top}]$$. The multivariate CLT gives $$\frac{1}{\sqrt n}\sum x_i\varepsilon_i \xrightarrow{d} N(0, \Omega)$$.

**Step 3.** $$\hat Q^{-1} \xrightarrow{p} Q^{-1}$$ (Exercise 6).

**Step 4.** Slutsky: $$\sqrt n(\hat\beta - \beta) \xrightarrow{d} Q^{-1}Z$$ with $$Z \sim N(0, \Omega)$$.

**Step 5.** By Day 22, $$\operatorname{Var}(Q^{-1}Z) = Q^{-1}\Omega Q^{-1}$$ ($$Q^{-1}$$ is symmetric). So the limit is $$N(0, Q^{-1}\Omega Q^{-1})$$. $$\blacksquare$$

</details>

### Exercise 8 · Homoskedasticity collapses the sandwich

Show that if $$E[\varepsilon^2 \mid x] = \sigma^2$$ then $$Q^{-1}\Omega Q^{-1} = \sigma^2Q^{-1}$$.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

By the LIE, $$\Omega = E[E[\varepsilon^2 \mid x]xx^{\top}] = \sigma^2E[xx^{\top}] = \sigma^2Q$$. So $$Q^{-1}\sigma^2QQ^{-1} = \sigma^2Q^{-1}$$.

</details>

### Exercise 9 · The linear probability model is heteroskedastic

For a binary outcome with $$P(y = 1 \mid x) = x^{\top}\beta$$, show $$\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon \mid x) = x^{\top}\beta(1 - x^{\top}\beta)$$.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** $$\varepsilon = y - x^{\top}\beta$$ and $$y \mid x$$ is Bernoulli($$p(x)$$), so $$\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon \mid x) = \operatorname{Var}(y \mid x) = p(x)(1 - p(x))$$, which varies with $$x$$.

**Step 2.** Robust SEs are mandatory for linear probability models.

</details>

### Exercise 10 · Robust SE of a difference in means

In $$y = \beta_0 + \beta_1T + \varepsilon$$, show that the HC0 variance of $$\hat\beta_1$$ equals $$\hat\sigma_1^2/n_1 + \hat\sigma_0^2/n_0$$ with plug-in (divide-by-$$n_g$$) group variances.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** By FWL (Day 28), the slope uses the demeaned treatment $$\tilde T_i = T_i - p$$, where $$p = n_1/n$$: $$\hat\beta_1 = \sum\tilde T_iy_i/\sum\tilde T_i^2$$. Its HC0 variance is $$\sum\tilde T_i^2e_i^2\big/\big(\sum\tilde T_i^2\big)^2$$.

**Step 2.** Ingredients: $$\tilde T_i = 1 - p$$ for treated and $$-p$$ for controls; $$e_i = y_i - \bar y_{g(i)}$$ (deviation from own group mean); $$\sum\tilde T_i^2 = n_1(1 - p)^2 + n_0p^2 = np(1 - p)$$.

**Step 3.** Numerator, writing $$\hat\sigma_g^2 = \frac{1}{n_g}\sum_{i \in g}e_i^2$$: $$(1 - p)^2n_1\hat\sigma_1^2 + p^2n_0\hat\sigma_0^2$$.

**Step 4.** Divide by $$n^2p^2(1 - p)^2$$ and use $$n_1 = np$$, $$n_0 = n(1 - p)$$:

$$
\frac{(1 - p)^2\,np\,\hat\sigma_1^2}{n^2p^2(1 - p)^2} + \frac{p^2\,n(1 - p)\,\hat\sigma_0^2}{n^2p^2(1 - p)^2} = \frac{\hat\sigma_1^2}{np} + \frac{\hat\sigma_0^2}{n(1 - p)} = \frac{\hat\sigma_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{\hat\sigma_0^2}{n_0}.
$$

**Answer:** the robust SE of a regression on a treatment dummy is Neyman's unequal-variance SE from Day 9.

</details>

## Part 3 · Applied

### Exercise 11 · Consumption, income and heteroskedasticity

In household data, consumption varies much more among high-income than low-income households. Model this as $$c_i = 1 + y_i + \varepsilon_i$$ with $$\operatorname{sd}(\varepsilon_i \mid y_i) = |y_i|$$ (income $$y$$ standardised). Predict whether classical SEs for the marginal propensity to consume over- or under-state its uncertainty, and check by simulation (code below).

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** The slope is driven by households far from mean income, where $$(y_i - \bar y)^2$$ is large.

**Step 2.** Here the error variance is largest exactly for those households.

**Step 3.** The classical formula uses one average error variance for everyone, so it ignores that the influential observations are also the noisiest.

**Answer:** classical SEs **understate** the uncertainty. In the simulation, classical 95% intervals cover only about 76% of the time, while HC1 robust intervals cover about 94.5%.

</details>

### Exercise 12 · The Moulton factor

The regressor is constant within clusters of size $$m = 50$$ and the error's intra-cluster correlation is 0.1. By how much are classical SEs too small?

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Moulton factor for the variance: $$1 + (m - 1)\rho_x\rho_\varepsilon = 1 + 49(1)(0.1) = 5.9$$.

**Step 2.** SE factor: $$\sqrt{5.9} \approx 2.43$$.

**Answer:** classical SEs are about 2.4 times too small: a reported $$t = 4$$ might really be $$t \approx 1.6$$.

</details>

### Exercise 13 · A joint Wald test

Two coefficients are estimated at $$(0.3, 0.2)$$ with robust covariance $$\operatorname{diag}(0.01, 0.01)$$. Test that both are zero.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** $$W = \hat\beta^{\top}V^{-1}\hat\beta$$ with $$V = \operatorname{diag}(0.01, 0.01)$$, so $$V^{-1} = \operatorname{diag}(100, 100)$$.

**Step 2.** $$W = 100(0.3^2) + 100(0.2^2) = 9 + 4 = 13$$.

**Step 3.** Reference: $$\chi^2_2$$, 5% critical value 5.99.

**Answer:** $$13 > 5.99$$: reject that both coefficients are zero. In general $$W = (R\hat\beta - r)^{\top}(RVR^{\top})^{-1}(R\hat\beta - r)$$ with $$q$$ degrees of freedom.

</details>

### Exercise 14 · Few clusters

A state-level policy is studied with 6 treated and 44 control states. Why can cluster-robust SEs mislead, and what do practitioners do?

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** The cluster-robust "meat" is a sum over clusters; it is reliable only with many clusters, especially many *treated* ones.

**Step 2.** With 6 treated states, those few clusters' residuals dominate, so the variance estimate is noisy and typically biased downward.

**Step 3.** Remedies: the wild cluster bootstrap (with restricted residuals), randomisation inference across states, or synthetic-control methods (Phase 6).

**Answer:** standard cluster-robust SEs are too small with few treated clusters; use one of the remedies.

</details>

### Exercise 15 · Exogeneity versus orthogonality

The asymptotic results need only $$E[x\varepsilon] = 0$$. Why does causal interpretation usually require the stronger $$E[\varepsilon \mid x] = 0$$?

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** For the *best linear predictor* of $$y$$ given $$x$$, the error is defined so that $$E[x\varepsilon] = 0$$; OLS always estimates those projection coefficients consistently.

**Step 2.** A causal reading needs a *structural* error: all other causes of $$y$$.

**Step 3.** $$E[\varepsilon \mid x] = 0$$ for that error says the regressors are unrelated to every omitted cause: the identifying assumption.

**Answer:** asymptotic theory delivers precision for whatever $$\hat\beta$$ estimates; whether that is a causal effect depends on identification.

</details>

## Verify in Python

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(29)
n, reps, beta = 200, 4000, 1.0
cover_cl = cover_hc = 0
for _ in range(reps):
    x = rng.normal(size=n); y = 1 + beta * x + np.abs(x) * rng.normal(size=n)
    X = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), x]); XtXi = np.linalg.inv(X.T @ X)
    b = XtXi @ X.T @ y; e = y - X @ b
    v_cl = (e @ e / (n - 2)) * XtXi[1, 1]
    v_hc = (XtXi @ (X.T * e**2) @ X @ XtXi)[1, 1] * n / (n - 2)       # HC1
    cover_cl += abs(b[1] - beta) <= 1.96 * np.sqrt(v_cl)
    cover_hc += abs(b[1] - beta) <= 1.96 * np.sqrt(v_hc)
print(f"95% CI coverage: classical {cover_cl / reps:.3f}, robust HC1 {cover_hc / reps:.3f}")

x = np.array([1, 2, 3.]); e = np.array([0.5, -1, 0.5])
print("classical:", round((e @ e / 2) / (x @ x), 4), " HC0:", round((x**2 * e**2).sum() / (x @ x)**2, 4))
print("Wald:", 0.3**2 / 0.01 + 0.2**2 / 0.01, " Moulton SE factor:", round(np.sqrt(1 + 49 * 0.1), 2))
```

## Key takeaways

- OLS is consistent and asymptotically normal under weak conditions; its variance is a sandwich.
- Use heteroskedasticity-robust SEs by default, and cluster at the level of treatment assignment.
- Asymptotic theory tells you how precise $$\hat\beta$$ is, not whether it is causal.
