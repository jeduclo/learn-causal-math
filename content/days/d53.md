## Theory

### A catalogue of controls

Let $$X$$ be the treatment and $$Y$$ the outcome. For a candidate control $$Z$$ (and possibly unobserved $$U$$):

| Structure | Verdict | Why |
|---|---|---|
| $$X \leftarrow Z \to Y$$ | **good** | blocks a back-door path |
| $$X \leftarrow Z \leftarrow U \to Y$$ | **good** | blocks the path through $$U$$ (a proxy) |
| $$Z \to Y$$ only | **neutral, helps precision** | explains outcome variance |
| $$Z \to X$$ only | **neutral, hurts precision** | removes treatment variation |
| $$Z \to X$$, $$X \leftarrow U \to Y$$ | **bad: bias amplification** | removes clean variation, keeps confounded variation |
| $$X \to Z \to Y$$ | **bad** (for the total effect) | blocks part of the effect |
| $$X \to Z \leftarrow Y$$, or $$X \to Z \leftarrow U \to Y$$ | **bad** | conditioning on a collider |
| $$X \leftarrow U_1 \to Z \leftarrow U_2 \to Y$$ | **bad: M-bias** | pre-treatment collider |
| $$Y \to Z$$ | **bad** | selection on the outcome |

"Pre-treatment" is not the same as "safe": the M-structure collider can be measured before treatment.

### Precision in a linear model

In $$Y = \tau X + \boldsymbol\gamma^\top\mathbf Z + \varepsilon$$ estimated by OLS, Frisch–Waugh–Lovell gives

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\hat\tau) \approx \frac{\sigma^2_{Y \mid X, \mathbf Z}}{n\,\operatorname{Var}(X \mid \mathbf Z)}.
$$

- An outcome predictor shrinks the numerator: more precision.
- A treatment predictor shrinks the denominator: less precision (the variance inflation factor $$1/(1 - R^2_{X \mid \mathbf Z})$$).

### Choosing among admissible sets

Several sets may satisfy the back-door criterion. In linear models the asymptotically most precise one is the **optimal adjustment set**: roughly, the parents of $$Y$$ (and of mediators on the causal path) that are not themselves descendants of $$X$$, excluding $$X$$. Practical rule: prefer confounders close to the outcome over confounders close to the treatment.

### The Table 2 fallacy

A regression designed to estimate the effect of $$X$$ has coefficients on the controls too. They are generally **not** causal effects of the controls: $$X$$ may be a mediator or collider for them. Report only the coefficient the design was built for.

## Part 1 · Warm-up

### Exercise 1 · Classify the controls

For the effect of $$X$$ on $$Y$$, classify $$Z$$: (a) $$Z \to X$$, $$Z \to Y$$; (b) $$X \to Z$$, $$Y \to Z$$; (c) $$Z \to Y$$ only; (d) $$Z \to X$$ only, no confounding; (e) $$X \to Z \to Y$$.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** (a) Confounder: good.

**Step 2.** (b) Collider: bad.

**Step 3.** (c) Outcome predictor: neutral, improves precision.

**Step 4.** (d) Instrument-like: neutral for bias, reduces precision.

**Step 5.** (e) Mediator: bad for the total effect.

</details>

### Exercise 2 · Precision from an outcome predictor

$$Y = \tau X + 2Z + \varepsilon$$, with $$Z \perp\!\!\!\perp X$$, $$\operatorname{Var}(Z) = 1$$, $$\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon) = 1$$. By what factor does including $$Z$$ reduce the standard error of $$\hat\tau$$?

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Without $$Z$$: residual variance $$4\operatorname{Var}(Z) + 1 = 5$$.

**Step 2.** With $$Z$$: residual variance 1.

**Step 3.** $$\operatorname{Var}(X \mid Z) = \operatorname{Var}(X)$$ since they are independent, so the denominator is unchanged.

**Answer:** variance falls by a factor of 5; the SE by $$\sqrt5 \approx 2.24$$.

</details>

### Exercise 3 · Precision lost to an instrument

$$X = Z + u$$ with $$\operatorname{Var}(Z) = \operatorname{Var}(u) = 1$$, and $$Z$$ affects $$Y$$ only through $$X$$ (no confounding). By what factor does controlling for $$Z$$ raise $$\operatorname{Var}(\hat\tau)$$?

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** $$\operatorname{Var}(X) = 2$$ and $$\operatorname{Var}(X \mid Z) = \operatorname{Var}(u) = 1$$.

**Step 2.** The residual variance of $$Y$$ is unchanged (Z has no direct effect on $$Y$$).

**Answer:** the variance doubles ($$R^2_{X \mid Z} = 0.5$$); the SE rises by $$\sqrt2$$.

</details>

### Exercise 4 · M-bias

In $$U_1 \to X$$, $$U_1 \to M$$, $$U_2 \to M$$, $$U_2 \to Y$$, $$X \to Y$$, with $$U_1, U_2$$ unobserved, is $$\varnothing$$ admissible? Is $$\{M\}$$?

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** The only back-door path is $$X \leftarrow U_1 \to M \leftarrow U_2 \to Y$$, with a collider at $$M$$.

**Step 2.** Given $$\varnothing$$ it is blocked: $$\varnothing$$ is admissible.

**Step 3.** Given $$M$$ it opens: $$\{M\}$$ is not admissible, although $$M$$ is pre-treatment and correlated with both $$X$$ and $$Y$$.

</details>

### Exercise 5 · Controlling for a consequence of the outcome

Why is it a mistake to control for $$D$$ when $$Y \to D$$ (for example, $$D$$ = hospital discharge diagnosis, $$Y$$ = disease severity)?

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** $$X \to Y \to D$$: $$D$$ is a descendant of $$X$$, violating the back-door condition.

**Step 2.** Conditioning on $$D$$ partially conditions on $$Y$$ itself: among units with the same $$D$$, variation in $$Y$$ is restricted, which attenuates (or distorts) the estimated effect.

**Answer:** a descendant of the outcome is a bad control.

</details>

## Part 2 · Proofs

### Exercise 6 · The variance formula

In $$Y = \tau X + \boldsymbol\gamma^\top\mathbf Z + \varepsilon$$ with homoskedastic $$\varepsilon$$, show $$\operatorname{Var}(\hat\tau \mid X, \mathbf Z) = \sigma^2/\sum_i \tilde X_i^2$$, where $$\tilde X$$ is $$X$$ residualised on $$\mathbf Z$$, and conclude the approximation in the theory.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** FWL: $$\hat\tau = \sum_i \tilde X_i Y_i/\sum_i \tilde X_i^2$$.

**Step 2.** Substitute $$Y_i = \tau X_i + \boldsymbol\gamma^\top\mathbf Z_i + \varepsilon_i$$; since $$\tilde X$$ is orthogonal to $$\mathbf Z$$ and $$\sum \tilde X_i X_i = \sum \tilde X_i^2$$, $$\hat\tau = \tau + \sum \tilde X_i\varepsilon_i/\sum \tilde X_i^2$$.

**Step 3.** $$\operatorname{Var}(\hat\tau \mid X, \mathbf Z) = \sigma^2\sum \tilde X_i^2/(\sum \tilde X_i^2)^2 = \sigma^2/\sum \tilde X_i^2$$.

**Step 4.** $$\sum \tilde X_i^2 \approx n\operatorname{Var}(X \mid \mathbf Z)$$ for large $$n$$. $$\blacksquare$$

</details>

### Exercise 7 · Bias amplification

Let $$X = aZ + bU + e_X$$ and $$Y = \tau X + cU + e_Y$$, with $$Z, U, e_X, e_Y$$ independent, $$\operatorname{Var}(Z) = \operatorname{Var}(U) = 1$$, $$\operatorname{Var}(e_X) = s^2$$, and $$U$$ unobserved. Show the bias of $$\hat\tau$$ is $$cb/(a^2 + b^2 + s^2)$$ without $$Z$$ and $$cb/(b^2 + s^2)$$ with $$Z$$.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Without $$Z$$: the slope is $$\tau + c\operatorname{Cov}(X, U)/\operatorname{Var}(X) = \tau + cb/(a^2 + b^2 + s^2)$$ (Day 50, Exercise 8).

**Step 2.** With $$Z$$: by FWL, use $$\tilde X = X - aZ = bU + e_X$$.

**Step 3.** Slope $$= \operatorname{Cov}(\tilde X, Y)/\operatorname{Var}(\tilde X)$$, and $$\operatorname{Cov}(\tilde X, Y) = \tau\operatorname{Var}(\tilde X) + cb$$.

**Step 4.** So the bias is $$cb/(b^2 + s^2)$$, which is larger: controlling for $$Z$$ removed clean variation ($$a^2$$) from the denominator. $$\blacksquare$$

</details>

### Exercise 8 · Outcome predictors do not change the estimand

Assume $$\mathbf Z$$ is admissible and $$W$$ is independent of $$(X, \mathbf Z)$$ and affects only $$Y$$. Prove that $$\mathbf Z \cup \{W\}$$ is admissible.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Condition 1: $$W$$ has no parents among the descendants of $$X$$ (it is independent of $$X$$ with arrows only into $$Y$$), so it is not a descendant of $$X$$.

**Step 2.** Condition 2: every path through $$W$$ is $$\cdots Y \leftarrow W$$ with $$W$$ as an endpoint-adjacent node; $$W$$ has no other neighbours, so no back-door path from $$X$$ passes through $$W$$.

**Step 3.** Conditioning on $$W$$ cannot open a path: $$W$$ is not a collider or a descendant of one on any $$X$$–$$Y$$ path.

**Step 4.** Paths blocked by $$\mathbf Z$$ remain blocked. $$\blacksquare$$

</details>

### Exercise 9 · Controlling for a confounded mediator

Let $$M = aX + U + e_M$$ and $$Y = dX + bM + cU + e_Y$$, with $$X, U, e_M, e_Y$$ independent, $$\operatorname{Var}(U) = v_U$$, $$\operatorname{Var}(e_M) = v_M$$. Show that the coefficient on $$X$$ in the regression of $$Y$$ on $$(X, M)$$ is $$d - cak$$, with $$k = v_U/(v_U + v_M)$$.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Given $$X$$ and $$M$$, we know $$U + e_M = M - aX$$. By normal (or linear) projection, $$E[U \mid X, M] = k(M - aX)$$.

**Step 2.** $$E[Y \mid X, M] = dX + bM + cE[U \mid X, M] = dX + bM + ck(M - aX)$$.

**Step 3.** Collect terms: $$(d - cak)X + (b + ck)M$$.

**Answer:** the coefficient is the direct effect $$d$$ only if $$c = 0$$ (no mediator–outcome confounding). Otherwise $$M$$ is a collider on $$X \to M \leftarrow U \to Y$$. $$\blacksquare$$

</details>

### Exercise 10 · The size of M-bias

In Exercise 4 with linear equations $$X = \alpha U_1 + e_X$$, $$M = U_1 + U_2 + e_M$$, $$Y = \tau X + \beta U_2 + e_Y$$ (all disturbances independent with variance 1), show that controlling for $$M$$ adds bias $$-\alpha\beta/(2\alpha^2 + 3)$$.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** $$E[Y \mid X, M] = \tau X + \beta E[U_2 \mid X, M]$$.

**Step 2.** $$\operatorname{Var}(X) = \alpha^2 + 1$$, $$\operatorname{Var}(M) = 3$$, $$\operatorname{Cov}(X, M) = \alpha$$; $$\operatorname{Cov}(U_2, X) = 0$$, $$\operatorname{Cov}(U_2, M) = 1$$.

**Step 3.** Projection coefficients: $$(0, 1)\begin{pmatrix} \alpha^2 + 1 & \alpha \\ \alpha & 3 \end{pmatrix}^{-1} = \dfrac{(-\alpha,\; \alpha^2 + 1)}{3(\alpha^2 + 1) - \alpha^2}$$.

**Step 4.** The coefficient on $$X$$ in $$E[U_2 \mid X, M]$$ is $$-\alpha/(2\alpha^2 + 3)$$.

**Answer:** bias $$-\alpha\beta/(2\alpha^2 + 3)$$; with $$\alpha = \beta = 1$$ it is −0.2. Usually modest, but real. $$\blacksquare$$

</details>

## Part 3 · Applied

### Exercise 11 · Medicaid expansion and mortality

You study the effect of a state's Medicaid expansion $$X$$ on county mortality $$Y$$. Classify each candidate control: (a) pre-expansion county poverty rate; (b) post-expansion insurance coverage; (c) post-expansion number of hospital closures, affected by both expansion and by local death rates through hospital finances; (d) county rainfall; (e) pre-expansion mortality trend.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** (a) Affects expansion decisions and mortality: confounder, include.

**Step 2.** (b) Mediator: expansion works through coverage. Exclude for the total effect.

**Step 3.** (c) Descendant of $$X$$ and of $$Y$$: collider-like and a descendant of the outcome. Exclude.

**Step 4.** (d) Unrelated to expansion; weakly predicts mortality at most. Harmless; include only if it explains outcome variance.

**Step 5.** (e) Strong predictor of mortality and plausibly of expansion: include (good for bias and precision).

</details>

### Exercise 12 · A cash-transfer RCT

In a randomised cash-transfer trial with 2,000 households, baseline consumption explains 60% of the variance of endline consumption. A field officer also proposes controlling for the household's distance to the payment point, which affected take-up but not consumption. What happens to the standard error of the effect of being offered the transfer with each control?

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Offer is randomised, so both controls leave the estimand unchanged.

**Step 2.** Baseline consumption: residual variance falls to 40%; SE falls by $$\sqrt{0.4} \approx 0.63$$, i.e. 37% smaller.

**Step 3.** Distance: it predicts take-up, not the random offer itself, and not consumption; it neither helps nor hurts the offer coefficient (beyond a tiny degrees-of-freedom cost).

**Answer:** control for baseline consumption; distance is irrelevant for the intention-to-treat effect. (If the treatment were take-up rather than the offer, distance would be an instrument and should not be a control.)

</details>

### Exercise 13 · Bias amplification in returns to schooling

Schooling $$S = 0.8D + 1.0A + e_S$$, where $$D$$ is distance to the nearest college (standardised, negative effect coded positive), $$A$$ unobserved ability (variance 1), $$\operatorname{Var}(e_S) = 1$$; log wage $$Y = 0.08S + 0.10A + e_Y$$. Compute the ability bias with and without controlling for $$D$$.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Use Exercise 7 with $$a = 0.8$$, $$b = 1$$, $$s^2 = 1$$, $$c = 0.10$$.

**Step 2.** Without $$D$$: $$0.10 \times 1/(0.64 + 1 + 1) = 0.10/2.64 \approx 0.038$$.

**Step 3.** With $$D$$: $$0.10/(1 + 1) = 0.050$$.

**Answer:** controlling for distance raises the bias from 3.8 to 5.0 points (estimated return 11.8% to 13.0% instead of 8%). Distance belongs in an IV analysis (Phase 5), not in the control list.

</details>

### Exercise 14 · Choosing the most precise admissible set

In a smoking-cessation study, $$A$$ (age) $$\to X$$ (counselling), $$A \to B$$ (baseline lung function), $$B \to Y$$ (lung function at one year), $$X \to Y$$. Both $$\{A\}$$ and $$\{B\}$$ are admissible. In the simulation below, which gives the smaller standard error, and why?

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Back-door path: $$X \leftarrow A \to B \to Y$$, blocked by $$A$$ (fork) or by $$B$$ (chain).

**Step 2.** $$B$$ is a parent of $$Y$$: controlling for it removes the large outcome variance it explains, shrinking the numerator of the variance formula.

**Step 3.** $$B$$ is only a noisy proxy for $$A$$, so $$\operatorname{Var}(X \mid B) > \operatorname{Var}(X \mid A)$$: more treatment variation is kept, enlarging the denominator.

**Step 4.** $$A$$ does the opposite on both counts: it predicts $$X$$ strongly and $$Y$$ only through $$B$$.

**Answer:** $$\{B\}$$ is more precise; the simulation shows an SE ratio of about 0.38. Prefer confounders near the outcome.

</details>

### Exercise 15 · The Table 2 fallacy

A paper estimates the effect of air pollution $$P$$ on asthma admissions $$Y$$, adjusting for temperature $$T$$ and income $$I$$ (both confounders; temperature also affects pollution). Its table reports coefficients for $$P$$, $$T$$ and $$I$$. A journalist writes "temperature has no effect on asthma once pollution is accounted for". What is wrong?

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** The regression was designed so that $$\{T, I\}$$ is an admissible set for $$P$$.

**Step 2.** For temperature, pollution is a **mediator** ($$T \to P \to Y$$). Conditioning on $$P$$ blocks that channel, so the $$T$$ coefficient is at most a direct effect, and possibly confounded by variables not needed for $$P$$.

**Answer:** the $$T$$ coefficient is not the total effect of temperature; only the coefficient on $$P$$ has the causal interpretation the design supports.

</details>

## Verify in Python

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(53)
def coef_se(y, X):
    X = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(y))] + X)
    b, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(X, y, rcond=None); r = y - X @ b
    V = r @ r / (len(y) - X.shape[1]) * np.linalg.inv(X.T @ X)
    return b[1], np.sqrt(V[1, 1])

n = 200_000
# Exercise 7 / 13: bias amplification
D = rng.normal(size=n); A = rng.normal(size=n)
S = .8 * D + A + rng.normal(size=n); Y = .08 * S + .10 * A + rng.normal(size=n)
print("schooling: no control %.4f, control for distance %.4f (truth 0.08)" % (coef_se(Y, [S])[0], coef_se(Y, [S, D])[0]))

# Exercise 9: confounded mediator (a=1, b=1, c=1, d=0.5, vU=vM=1 -> coef 0.5 - 0.5 = 0)
X = rng.normal(size=n); U = rng.normal(size=n)
M = X + U + rng.normal(size=n); Y = .5 * X + M + U + rng.normal(size=n)
print("coef on X given M: %.3f (direct effect 0.5, formula 0.0)" % coef_se(Y, [X, M])[0])

# Exercise 10: M-bias with alpha = beta = 1 (tau = 1)
U1, U2 = rng.normal(size=n), rng.normal(size=n)
X = U1 + rng.normal(size=n); M = U1 + U2 + rng.normal(size=n); Y = X + U2 + rng.normal(size=n)
print("M-bias: without M %.3f, with M %.3f (bias -0.2)" % (coef_se(Y, [X])[0], coef_se(Y, [X, M])[0]))

# Exercise 14: precision of two admissible sets, 400 patients, 2000 replications
est = {"A": [], "B": []}
for _ in range(2000):
    m = 400
    A = rng.normal(size=m); X = (1.5 * A + rng.normal(size=m) > 0).astype(float)
    B = A + rng.normal(size=m); Y = 1.0 * X + 2 * B + rng.normal(size=m)
    est["A"].append(coef_se(Y, [X, A])[0]); est["B"].append(coef_se(Y, [X, B])[0])
sa, sb = np.std(est["A"]), np.std(est["B"])
print(f"mean A {np.mean(est['A']):.3f}, B {np.mean(est['B']):.3f}; SD A {sa:.3f}, B {sb:.3f}, ratio {sb / sa:.2f}")
```

## Key takeaways

- Include confounders (or their proxies); add strong outcome predictors for precision.
- Leave out instruments, mediators, colliders (including pre-treatment M-colliders) and descendants of the outcome.
- Among admissible sets, those closer to the outcome are more precise; coefficients on controls are not causal by default.
