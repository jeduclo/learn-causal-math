## Theory

### Start from the variation

The first question is not "which estimator?" but "what moves treatment for reasons unrelated to outcomes?"

| Source of variation | Design | Estimand | Key assumption | Diagnostics |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| You randomise | RCT (Phase 3) | ATE (or ITT) | randomisation, no attrition bias | balance, attrition, compliance |
| A random or as-good-as-random nudge to take-up | IV (61–66) | LATE for compliers | independence, exclusion, monotonicity | first stage F, balance on Z, AR intervals, complier profile |
| A policy changes for some groups at some time | DiD / event study (67–72) | ATT for treated groups | parallel trends, no anticipation | pre-trends, placebo outcomes, sensitivity bounds, robust staggered estimators |
| A rule assigns treatment at a threshold | RD (73–77) | effect at the cutoff (compliers there if fuzzy) | continuity at the cutoff | density test, covariate balance, placebo cutoffs, bandwidth sensitivity |
| A rule changes the slope of a schedule | RKD (78) | marginal effect at the kink | smoothness | slope placebo, density derivative |
| None of the above is credible | bounds (79), sensitivity (95) | identified set | only what you state | transparency about assumptions |

### Estimands differ

Each design answers a question about a particular group: compliers with a lottery, treated states, students at a test-score cutoff. With heterogeneous effects, credible designs applied to the same policy can legitimately disagree. Before comparing numbers, compare estimands.

### Combining designs

- **Wald-DiD (fuzzy DiD):** if a policy raises take-up in treated groups, divide the DiD in outcomes by the DiD in take-up.
- **Difference in discontinuities:** if a jump already existed at a cutoff before a policy started applying there, subtract the old jump from the new one.
- **RD + bounds, IV + DiD, event studies with instruments**: assumptions combine, and so do diagnostics.

### A workflow

1. State the causal question and the estimand (whose effect, of what, on what, when).
2. Describe the assignment mechanism with institutional detail; draw the DAG.
3. Choose the design that matches the variation; state its assumptions plainly.
4. Run the diagnostics; report the reduced form or raw comparisons graphically.
5. Report estimates with robust inference and sensitivity analyses.
6. Discuss external validity: for whom does the estimate speak?

### Exercise 2 · What can be checked?

For IV, DiD and RD, name one implication of the key assumption that can be checked in data and one part that cannot.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** IV: balance of pre-determined covariates across instrument values (checkable); exclusion (not checkable).

**Step 2.** DiD: parallel pre-trends (checkable); parallel post-treatment trends (not).

**Step 3.** RD: density and covariate continuity (checkable); continuity of potential outcomes themselves (not directly).

</details>

### Exercise 3 · Three numbers for one programme

A school-meals programme is evaluated three ways: an RD at the eligibility income cutoff (+0.12 sd in test scores), a DiD across districts that adopted early (+0.08), and a lottery among oversubscribed schools (+0.15 per pupil fed). Should they agree?

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** RD: effect for pupils at the income cutoff.

**Step 2.** DiD: ATT for pupils in early-adopting districts (a mix of incomes).

**Step 3.** Lottery IV: LATE for pupils in oversubscribed schools who ate because their school won.

**Answer:** not necessarily; with heterogeneous effects they estimate different averages. Disagreement is informative about heterogeneity, provided each design passes its diagnostics.

</details>

### Exercise 4 · Name the estimand

Give the estimand of each: a sharp RD; a fuzzy RD; a Wald estimator with one-sided non-compliance; a 2×2 DiD; Lee bounds.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Sharp RD: ATE at the cutoff.

**Step 2.** Fuzzy RD: LATE for compliers at the cutoff.

**Step 3.** One-sided Wald: ATT (effect on the treated).

**Step 4.** 2×2 DiD: ATT for the treated group in the post period.

**Step 5.** Lee bounds: an interval for the effect on always-observed units.

</details>

### Exercise 5 · A diagnostics list

For a staggered DiD of a state paid-leave law, list four diagnostics.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Event-study pre-trends using a heterogeneity-robust estimator.

**Step 2.** Bacon decomposition of TWFE to see forbidden comparisons.

**Step 3.** Placebo outcomes (groups not covered by the law).

**Step 4.** Sensitivity to bounded violations of parallel trends (breakdown value).

</details>

## Part 2 · Proofs

### Exercise 6 · Wald-DiD

Suppose a reform raises take-up $$D$$ more in group 1 than in group 0, and $$Y = \alpha_g + \lambda_t + \tau D + \varepsilon$$ with a constant effect $$\tau$$ and parallel trends in $$\varepsilon$$. Prove that the DiD in $$Y$$ divided by the DiD in $$D$$ equals $$\tau$$.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Group-period means: $$\bar Y_{gt} = \alpha_g + \lambda_t + \tau\bar D_{gt} + \bar\varepsilon_{gt}$$.

**Step 2.** DiD in $$Y$$: the $$\alpha_g$$ and $$\lambda_t$$ cancel; parallel trends cancels the $$\varepsilon$$ terms; left with $$\tau\times$$ DiD in $$D$$.

**Step 3.** Divide (DiD in $$D$$ non-zero). $$\blacksquare$$ With heterogeneous effects, extra assumptions are needed for a LATE interpretation (de Chaisemartin and D'Haultfœuille).

</details>

### Exercise 7 · Difference in discontinuities

A cutoff at age 65 had a jump $$J_0$$ in employment before a pension reform (from other age-65 rules) and $$J_1$$ after the reform also started at 65. Show that $$J_1 - J_0$$ identifies the reform's effect at 65 if the other rules' jump is unchanged.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** $$J_0 = \delta$$ (other rules); $$J_1 = \delta + \tau$$ (other rules plus reform).

**Step 2.** $$J_1 - J_0 = \tau$$ when $$\delta$$ is the same in both periods. $$\blacksquare$$ This combines RD's local comparison with DiD's removal of a stable confounder.

</details>

### Exercise 8 · Placebo outcomes

Show that under parallel trends, a DiD on an outcome that treatment cannot affect has expectation zero, and explain what a non-zero placebo implies.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** For an unaffected outcome, $$Y(1) = Y(0)$$, so the ATT is zero.

**Step 2.** By Day 67, Exercise 9, the DiD equals ATT + trend bias = trend bias.

**Answer:** if parallel trends holds for the placebo, its DiD is zero in expectation; a non-zero placebo reveals differential trends, which may also affect the real outcome (if the outcomes share trend drivers). $$\blacksquare$$

</details>

### Exercise 9 · OLS or IV by mean squared error

OLS has bias $$b$$ and variance $$v_O$$; IV is unbiased with variance $$v_I > v_O$$. Show IV has lower MSE iff $$b^2 > v_I - v_O$$.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** $$\text{MSE}_{OLS} = b^2 + v_O$$; $$\text{MSE}_{IV} = v_I$$.

**Step 2.** $$v_I < b^2 + v_O \iff b^2 > v_I - v_O$$. $$\blacksquare$$ With a weak instrument, $$v_I$$ can be so large that a biased OLS is closer to the truth, but its bias is unknown, so IV's confidence interval is the honest summary.

</details>

### Exercise 10 · Reweighting for external validity

Suppose effects vary only with a covariate $$X$$: $$\tau(x)$$, and a study identifies $$\tau(x)$$ for each $$x$$ in its sample. Show the ATE for a target population with covariate distribution $$Q$$ is $$\int\tau(x)\,dQ(x)$$, and that the study's own average uses its own distribution $$P$$.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Target ATE: $$E_Q[Y(1) - Y(0)] = E_Q[E[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid X]] = E_Q[\tau(X)]$$.

**Step 2.** The study's average is $$E_P[\tau(X)]$$; they differ when $$P \ne Q$$ and $$\tau$$ varies.

**Answer:** reweighting the study's conditional effects to the target's covariate mix transports the estimate, if effects depend only on observed $$X$$ (and $$Q$$'s support is covered). $$\blacksquare$$

</details>

## Part 3 · Applied

### Exercise 11 · Minimum wages: two designs

One study compares contiguous border counties across state lines (DiD with county pairs); another uses the bunching of wages at the new minimum to count jobs lost below it. What does each assume, and why might they disagree?

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Border-pair DiD: parallel trends between neighbouring counties; estimates employment effects for those counties.

**Step 2.** Bunching: jobs that disappear below the new minimum versus excess jobs at and above it, relative to a counterfactual wage distribution; assumes the counterfactual distribution can be predicted (e.g., from other states).

**Answer:** they use different variation and estimands; disagreement may come from spillovers up the wage distribution or from local labour-market differences. Each paper's diagnostics, not its design label, decide credibility.

</details>

### Exercise 12 · Class size: RD versus an experiment

The Tennessee STAR experiment found that classes about 7 pupils smaller raised scores by about 0.2 sd; Maimonides' rule RD finds about 0.25 points per pupil on a different test in Israel. How should these be compared?

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Put both in comparable units (sd per pupil): STAR ≈ 0.03 sd per pupil; convert the RD estimate using the test's sd.

**Step 2.** Estimands differ: STAR's ATE for kindergarten to grade 3 in Tennessee; the RD's effect at the 40-pupil threshold in Israeli schools.

**Answer:** agreement in standardised units across designs increases confidence; differences may reflect age, baseline class size and context rather than bias.

</details>

### Exercise 13 · Compulsory schooling: IV or RD?

Evaluate two designs for the effect of an extra year of schooling: (a) IV using changes in compulsory-schooling laws across states and cohorts; (b) RD on school-entry birth-date cutoffs.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** (a) LATE for students who stay in school only because of the law (likely low-attainment); threats: other policies changing with the laws, weak first stages.

**Step 2.** (b) Compares children born just before and after the cutoff: they differ in school-starting age and in age at test, not only in years of schooling; exclusion is problematic for "years of schooling".

**Answer:** (a) speaks to compulsory schooling policy for dropouts; (b) is better suited to the effect of school-starting age.

</details>

### Exercise 14 · A staggered hospital payment reform

A country moves hospitals from fee-for-service to bundled payments, region by region over four years, plus one region that never switches. Design the evaluation.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Estimand: ATT on cost per episode and readmissions, by years since adoption.

**Step 2.** Design: group-time ATTs (Callaway–Sant'Anna) with never-treated and not-yet-treated regions; event-study aggregation over a balanced window.

**Step 3.** Diagnostics: pre-trends, placebo outcomes (conditions not covered by bundles), Bacon decomposition of any TWFE estimate, sensitivity to bounded violations.

**Step 4.** Inference clustered by region (few clusters: wild bootstrap or randomisation inference over adoption dates).

</details>

### Exercise 15 · One world, three estimands

In the simulation below, a programme's effect is larger for poorer households: $$\tau(x) = 2 - 1.5x$$ for a poverty score $$x \in [-1, 1]$$ (lower is poorer). Households with $$x < 0$$ are eligible; a lottery among eligible households offers places, and poorer households comply more. Compute the population ATE, the ATE among eligible households, the RD estimate at the eligibility cutoff (using a hypothetical universal take-up rule), and the lottery's LATE.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Population ATE: $$2 - 1.5E[x] = 2$$.

**Step 2.** Eligible ATE: $$2 - 1.5(-0.5) = 2.75$$.

**Step 3.** RD at the cutoff: $$\tau(0) = 2$$.

**Step 4.** Lottery LATE: compliers are poorer than the average eligible household, so the LATE exceeds 2.75 (the code finds about 2.9).

**Answer:** four correct numbers for four questions. Policy makers need the one matching the decision: expanding eligibility (RD-type), scaling up within the eligible (eligible ATE), or improving outreach (LATE-type).

</details>

## Verify in Python

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(80); n = 2_000_000
x = rng.uniform(-1, 1, n)
tau = 2 - 1.5 * x
y0 = 5 + x + rng.normal(0, 1, n)
eligible = x < 0
print(f"population ATE {tau.mean():.3f}   eligible ATE {tau[eligible].mean():.3f}")

# Sharp RD at the cutoff with universal take-up for the eligible
d_rd = eligible.astype(float); y = y0 + d_rd * tau
def side(xx, yy, h=.2):
    w = np.clip(1 - np.abs(xx) / h, 0, None); m = w > 0
    X = np.column_stack([np.ones(m.sum()), xx[m]]); W = w[m]
    return np.linalg.solve(X.T @ (W[:, None] * X), X.T @ (W * yy[m]))[0]
rd = side(-x[eligible], y[eligible]) - side(x[~eligible], y[~eligible])
print(f"RD at cutoff {rd:.3f} (tau(0) = 2)")

# Lottery among the eligible; poorer households comply more
e = eligible
Z = rng.random(n) < .5
comply = rng.random(n) < (0.3 - 0.6 * x)          # poorer households (x near -1) comply with prob 0.9, those near 0 with 0.3
D = e & Z & comply
yL = y0 + D * tau
wald = (yL[e & Z].mean() - yL[e & ~Z].mean()) / (D[e & Z].mean() - D[e & ~Z].mean())
print(f"lottery LATE {wald:.3f}  (true complier average {tau[e & comply].mean():.3f})")

# Exercise 9: OLS vs IV by MSE
b, vO, vI = .05, .0001, .0016
print("IV preferred by MSE:", b ** 2 > vI - vO)
```

## Key takeaways

- Start from the source of exogenous variation; it determines the design, its assumptions and its estimand.
- Every design has testable implications; run them, plot the raw comparisons and report robust inference.
- Credible designs can disagree because they target different people; designs can also be combined (Wald-DiD, difference in discontinuities) when one alone is not enough.
