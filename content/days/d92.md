## Theory

### The target

$$
\tau(x) = E[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid X = x] = \mu_1(x) - \mu_0(x)
$$

under unconfoundedness and overlap. We never observe $$Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)$$, so we cannot regress it on $$X$$ directly; meta-learners build substitutes.

### The learners

- **S-learner** ("single"): fit $$\hat f(x, d)$$ on $$(X, D)$$; $$\hat\tau(x) = \hat f(x, 1) - \hat f(x, 0)$$. Regularisation tends to shrink the role of $$D$$, biasing $$\hat\tau$$ towards zero or towards homogeneity.
- **T-learner** ("two"): fit $$\hat\mu_1$$ on treated, $$\hat\mu_0$$ on controls; $$\hat\tau = \hat\mu_1 - \hat\mu_0$$. Errors of two separately regularised fits add up, and a complicated $$\mu_0$$ produces spurious heterogeneity.
- **X-learner** (Künzel et al.): impute individual effects $$\tilde\tau_i^1 = Y_i - \hat\mu_0(X_i)$$ for treated and $$\tilde\tau_i^0 = \hat\mu_1(X_i) - Y_i$$ for controls; regress each on $$X$$ to get $$\hat g_1, \hat g_0$$; combine $$\hat\tau = e\,\hat g_0 + (1 - e)\,\hat g_1$$. It excels when one arm is small.
- **DR-learner** (Kennedy): regress the cross-fitted AIPW pseudo-outcome

$$
\hat\varphi_i = \hat\mu_1(X_i) - \hat\mu_0(X_i) + \frac{D_i(Y_i - \hat\mu_1(X_i))}{\hat e(X_i)} - \frac{(1 - D_i)(Y_i - \hat\mu_0(X_i))}{1 - \hat e(X_i)}
$$

on $$X$$. Its bias is a product of nuisance errors, but the pseudo-outcome is noisy when $$\hat e$$ is near 0 or 1, so a simple final stage often works best.
- **R-learner** (Nie and Wager): minimise the Robinson loss

$$
\hat\tau = \arg\min_\tau \sum_i\big[(Y_i - \hat\ell(X_i)) - \tau(X_i)(D_i - \hat e(X_i))\big]^2 + \Lambda(\tau),
$$

the heterogeneous version of the partialling-out score (Days 89–91).

### Evaluating CATE estimates

In simulations use the PEHE, $$\sqrt{E[(\hat\tau(X) - \tau(X))^2]}$$. In real data, use held-out checks: calibration of $$\hat\tau$$ against AIPW scores by quantile group, and the best linear predictor test (Day 93).

### Exercise 2 · X-learner combination

At $$x$$: $$e(x) = 0.1$$, $$\hat g_1(x) = 1.4$$ (from treated), $$\hat g_0(x) = 1.0$$ (from controls). Compute $$\hat\tau(x)$$ and explain the weights.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** $$\hat\tau = 0.1\times1.0 + 0.9\times1.4 = 1.36$$.

**Step 2.** $$\hat g_1$$ uses imputations $$Y - \hat\mu_0$$, where $$\hat\mu_0$$ is fitted on the large control group and is accurate; it gets weight $$1 - e = 0.9$$.

</details>

### Exercise 3 · DR pseudo-outcome

A treated unit has $$Y = 10$$, $$\hat\mu_1 = 8$$, $$\hat\mu_0 = 6$$, $$\hat e = 0.25$$. Compute $$\hat\varphi$$.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** $$\hat\varphi = 8 - 6 + (10 - 8)/0.25 = 2 + 8 = 10$$.

**Answer:** 10: individually very noisy, informative only on average.

</details>

### Exercise 4 · S-learner shrinkage

A lasso S-learner on $$(X, D, D\times X)$$ sets every interaction to zero. What CATE does it report?

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** $$\hat f(x, 1) - \hat f(x, 0)$$ equals the coefficient on $$D$$ for every $$x$$.

**Answer:** a constant effect: no heterogeneity, whatever the truth.

</details>

### Exercise 5 · PEHE

True CATE at three points: $$1, 2, 3$$; estimates $$1.5, 2, 2$$. Compute the PEHE.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Squared errors: $$0.25, 0, 1$$; mean $$0.4167$$.

**Answer:** PEHE $$= \sqrt{0.4167} = 0.645$$.

</details>

## Part 2 · Proofs

### Exercise 6 · Why we cannot regress on the individual effect

Show that regressing $$Y$$ on $$X$$ separately in each arm and differencing identifies $$\tau(x)$$ under unconfoundedness, but regressing $$DY/e - (1 - D)Y/(1 - e)$$ on $$X$$ also does, and explain why the second is noisier.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** $$E[Y \mid D = d, X] = E[Y(d) \mid X]$$ by unconfoundedness, so $$\mu_1 - \mu_0 = \tau$$.

**Step 2.** $$E[DY/e \mid X] = e\mu_1/e = \mu_1$$, similarly for the control term, so the IPW pseudo-outcome has conditional mean $$\tau(x)$$.

**Step 3.** Its conditional variance includes $$\mu_1^2(1 - e)/e$$-type terms that blow up for extreme $$e$$; the DR pseudo-outcome subtracts $$\hat\mu$$ to remove most of this. $$\blacksquare$$

</details>

### Exercise 7 · The DR pseudo-outcome is conditionally unbiased

At the true nuisances, show $$E[\varphi \mid X = x] = \tau(x)$$.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** $$E[D(Y - \mu_1) \mid X] = e(X)\cdot E[Y - \mu_1 \mid D = 1, X] = 0$$.

**Step 2.** Likewise for the control term.

**Step 3.** So $$E[\varphi \mid X] = \mu_1(X) - \mu_0(X) = \tau(X)$$. $$\blacksquare$$ With estimated nuisances the conditional bias is the product form of Day 89, Exercise 9, now pointwise in $$x$$.

</details>

### Exercise 8 · The R-loss is minimised at the CATE

With $$Y = \mu_0(X) + \tau(X)D + \varepsilon$$, $$\ell = E[Y \mid X]$$, show the population minimiser of $$E[(Y - \ell(X) - t(X)(D - e(X)))^2]$$ over functions $$t$$ is $$\tau$$.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** $$\ell = \mu_0 + \tau e$$, so $$Y - \ell = \tau(D - e) + \varepsilon$$.

**Step 2.** The loss is $$E[((\tau - t)(D - e) + \varepsilon)^2] = E[(\tau - t)^2e(1 - e)] + E[\varepsilon^2]$$ (cross term zero since $$E[\varepsilon \mid D, X] = 0$$).

**Step 3.** Minimised by $$t = \tau$$ wherever $$e(1 - e) > 0$$. $$\blacksquare$$

</details>

### Exercise 9 · X-learner imputations

Show that if $$\hat\mu_0 = \mu_0$$, then $$E[Y - \hat\mu_0(X) \mid X, D = 1] = \tau(X)$$.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** $$E[Y \mid X, D = 1] = \mu_1(X)$$.

**Step 2.** So the conditional mean is $$\mu_1 - \mu_0 = \tau$$. $$\blacksquare$$ Errors in $$\hat\mu_0$$ pass directly into $$\hat g_1$$, which is why the X-learner wants the control model fitted on the larger group.

</details>

### Exercise 10 · T-learner spurious heterogeneity

Suppose $$\tau(x) = c$$ is constant, and $$\hat\mu_1, \hat\mu_0$$ have errors $$b_1(x), b_0(x)$$. Show $$\hat\tau(x) - c = b_1(x) - b_0(x)$$ and explain why fitting the arms separately with different regularisation creates heterogeneity that is not there.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** $$\hat\tau = (\mu_1 + b_1) - (\mu_0 + b_0) = c + b_1 - b_0$$.

**Step 2.** A small treated arm is smoothed more heavily: $$b_1$$ is a crude approximation of the complicated $$\mu_0 + c$$, while $$b_0 \approx 0$$. The difference $$b_1 - b_0$$ inherits the shape of $$\mu_0$$. $$\blacksquare$$

</details>

## Part 3 · Applied

### Exercise 11 · Who gains from a cash-transfer pilot?

A pilot enrols about 11% of 2,000 households in a conditional cash transfer. School attendance depends on many household characteristics in complicated ways; the true effect rises linearly with the poverty index, $$\tau(x) = 1 + x_1$$. Compare meta-learners by PEHE (random forests throughout).

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** S-learner 0.49 and T-learner 0.54: the S-learner dampens heterogeneity; the T-learner's small treated-arm forest imports the shape of the baseline outcome.

**Step 2.** X-learner 0.22: it uses the accurate control model to impute effects for the small treated group.

**Step 3.** DR-learner with a forest final stage 0.71 (noisy pseudo-outcomes with $$1/\hat e \approx 10$$); with a linear final stage 0.18, recovering slope about 1.2 (truth 1).

**Answer:** with imbalanced arms and simple effects, prefer the X-learner or a DR/R-learner with a simple final stage.

</details>

### Exercise 12 · Choosing a learner for a vaccination reminder trial

A randomised trial ($$e = 0.5$$) of text reminders for flu vaccination has 50,000 participants. Which learner would you start with, and why?

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** With known, balanced propensity, DR pseudo-outcomes are stable ($$1/e = 2$$), and the large sample supports a flexible final stage.

**Answer:** a DR- or R-learner with a forest or boosting final stage, plus a T-learner as a check.

</details>

### Exercise 13 · Targeting a smoking-cessation subsidy

A CATE model ranks smokers by predicted effect of a subsidy. Why is it more defensible to report average effects by quintile of $$\hat\tau$$ than individual predictions?

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Individual CATE estimates are noisy; averaging AIPW scores within held-out quintile groups gives unbiased group effects with valid SEs.

**Step 2.** A rising pattern across quintiles is evidence that the model captures real heterogeneity.

</details>

### Exercise 14 · Spurious subgroup effects

A T-learner suggests that a nutrition programme helps urban children far more than rural ones, but rural children were only 5% of the treated. What check would you run?

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Re-estimate with an X- or DR-learner, and compute held-out AIPW averages for urban and rural treated children with SEs.

**Step 2.** If the gap shrinks or the CI is wide, the T-learner pattern likely reflects the poorly fitted small arm (Exercise 10).

</details>

### Exercise 15 · Plain-language summary

Explain to a ministry why the X-learner is a good choice when only a small share received a programme.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** We learn well what happens without the programme, because most people did not receive it.

**Step 2.** For each participant we compare their actual outcome with this well-estimated no-programme prediction, and then learn how these gains vary with characteristics.

</details>

## Verify in Python

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestRegressor, RandomForestClassifier
from sklearn.model_selection import KFold

rng = np.random.default_rng(92)
n, p = 2000, 6
X = rng.uniform(-1, 1, size=(n, p))
e = np.clip(.1 + .1 * X[:, 0], .02, .98); D = rng.binomial(1, e)
mu0 = 2 * np.sin(3 * X[:, 0]) + 2 * X[:, 1] ** 2 + X[:, 2]
Y = mu0 + (1 + X[:, 0]) * D + .5 * rng.normal(size=n)
Xt = rng.uniform(-1, 1, size=(5000, p)); taut = 1 + Xt[:, 0]
R = lambda: RandomForestRegressor(200, min_samples_leaf=5, n_jobs=-1, random_state=0)
pehe = lambda t: np.sqrt(np.mean((t - taut) ** 2))
one, zero = np.ones(5000), np.zeros(5000)

s = R().fit(np.column_stack([X, D]), Y)
tS = s.predict(np.column_stack([Xt, one])) - s.predict(np.column_stack([Xt, zero]))
f1, f0 = R().fit(X[D == 1], Y[D == 1]), R().fit(X[D == 0], Y[D == 0])
tT = f1.predict(Xt) - f0.predict(Xt)
g1 = R().fit(X[D == 1], Y[D == 1] - f0.predict(X[D == 1]))
g0 = R().fit(X[D == 0], f1.predict(X[D == 0]) - Y[D == 0])
et = .1 + .1 * Xt[:, 0]
tX = et * g0.predict(Xt) + (1 - et) * g1.predict(Xt)

phi = np.empty(n)                                   # cross-fitted DR pseudo-outcomes
for tr, te in KFold(5, shuffle=True, random_state=0).split(X):
    t1, t0 = tr[D[tr] == 1], tr[D[tr] == 0]
    a1, a0 = R().fit(X[t1], Y[t1]).predict(X[te]), R().fit(X[t0], Y[t0]).predict(X[te])
    eh = np.clip(RandomForestClassifier(200, min_samples_leaf=20, n_jobs=-1, random_state=0)
                 .fit(X[tr], D[tr]).predict_proba(X[te])[:, 1], .02, .98)
    phi[te] = a1 - a0 + D[te] * (Y[te] - a1) / eh - (1 - D[te]) * (Y[te] - a0) / (1 - eh)
tDR = R().fit(X, phi).predict(Xt)
b = np.linalg.lstsq(np.column_stack([np.ones(n), X]), phi, rcond=None)[0]
tDRl = np.column_stack([np.ones(5000), Xt]) @ b

print(f"treated share {D.mean():.3f}")
for k, v in {"S": tS, "T": tT, "X": tX, "DR-forest": tDR, "DR-linear": tDRl}.items():
    print(f"{k:10s} PEHE {pehe(v):.3f}")
print("DR-linear intercept and slope on x1:", b[:2].round(3))
print("Ex 5 PEHE:", round(float(np.sqrt(np.mean((np.array([1.5, 2, 2]) - np.array([1, 2, 3])) ** 2))), 3))
```

## Key takeaways

- The CATE is a difference of conditional means; meta-learners build it from ordinary regressions.
- S-learners shrink heterogeneity away; T-learners invent it when arms are fitted with different accuracy; X-learners shine with imbalanced arms.
- DR- and R-learners are orthogonal (bias is a product of nuisance errors); keep their final stage simple when propensities are extreme.
