## Theory

### Potential outcomes under interference

With interference, unit $$i$$'s outcome depends on the whole assignment vector: $$Y_i(\mathbf d)$$. This is unmanageable without structure.

- **Partial interference:** units are grouped (villages, schools, households); spillovers occur within but not between groups.
- **Exposure mapping:** outcomes depend on $$\mathbf d$$ only through $$(d_i, e_i)$$, where $$e_i$$ summarises neighbours' treatment, e.g. the number or share of treated neighbours.

### Two-stage randomisation

Hudgens and Halloran (2008): first randomise each cluster to a **saturation** $$\alpha$$ (share to be treated), then randomise individuals within the cluster. Define, at saturation $$\alpha$$:

| Effect | Definition | Question |
|---|---|---|
| Direct $$DE(\alpha)$$ | $$E[Y(1) \mid \alpha] - E[Y(0) \mid \alpha]$$ | Effect of own treatment, holding the village saturation fixed |
| Indirect $$IE(\alpha)$$ | $$E[Y(0) \mid \alpha] - E[Y(0) \mid 0]$$ | Spillover on untreated units |
| Total $$TE(\alpha)$$ | $$E[Y(1) \mid \alpha] - E[Y(0) \mid 0]$$ | Treated in a treated village versus a pure control |
| Overall $$OE(\alpha)$$ | $$E[\bar Y \mid \alpha] - E[\bar Y \mid 0]$$ | Policy effect of treating share $$\alpha$$ |

Pure control clusters ($$\alpha = 0$$) are essential for indirect effects. Inference is at the cluster level.

### What goes wrong without the design

A standard individually randomised trial compares treated and control units that both enjoy spillovers, so it estimates the direct effect at the trial's saturation, which understates the policy effect when spillovers are positive; pooling across saturations mixes different estimands.

### Network interference

With a network, define exposures (e.g. "treated, at least one treated friend"). Under randomisation, each unit's probability $$\pi_i(e)$$ of receiving exposure $$e$$ is computable, and the **Horvitz–Thompson** estimator

$$
\hat\mu(e) = \frac1n\sum_i\frac{\mathbf 1\{E_i = e\}Y_i}{\pi_i(e)}
$$

is unbiased for the average potential outcome under exposure $$e$$ (Aronow and Samii, 2017).

### Exercise 2 · Effects from four means

Mean health index: treated in 75%-saturation villages 7.43; untreated in those villages 6.71; untreated in pure control villages 5.00. Compute $$DE$$, $$IE$$, $$TE$$ at $$\alpha = 0.75$$.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** $$DE = 7.43 - 6.71 = 0.72$$.

**Step 2.** $$IE = 6.71 - 5.00 = 1.71$$.

**Step 3.** $$TE = 7.43 - 5.00 = 2.43$$ $$= DE + IE$$.

</details>

### Exercise 3 · Overall effect

Continuing Exercise 2, compute $$OE(0.75)$$.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Village mean at $$\alpha = 0.75$$: $$0.75\times7.43 + 0.25\times6.71 = 7.25$$.

**Answer:** $$OE = 7.25 - 5.00 = 2.25$$.

</details>

### Exercise 4 · Exposure probabilities

In a school of 4 children, 2 are randomly treated. What is the probability that a given child is untreated and has exactly one treated classmate?

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Untreated means both treated slots go to the other 3: $$\binom32/\binom42 = 3/6 = 0.5$$.

**Step 2.** Then exactly 2 of the other 3 are treated (never exactly 1).

**Answer:** probability 0: with fixed numbers, some exposures are impossible; positivity matters for exposure mappings.

</details>

### Exercise 5 · Unit of inference

Why are standard errors computed at the village level in a saturation design?

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Answer:** saturations are assigned to villages, and outcomes within a village are correlated through spillovers and common shocks; the village is the independent unit.

</details>

## Part 2 · Proofs

### Exercise 6 · Decomposition

Show $$TE(\alpha) = DE(\alpha) + IE(\alpha)$$.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** $$DE + IE = [E[Y(1) \mid \alpha] - E[Y(0) \mid \alpha]] + [E[Y(0) \mid \alpha] - E[Y(0) \mid 0]]$$.

**Step 2.** The middle terms cancel, leaving $$TE(\alpha)$$. $$\blacksquare$$

</details>

### Exercise 7 · The overall effect

Show $$OE(\alpha) = \alpha\,TE(\alpha) + (1 - \alpha)\,IE(\alpha)$$.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** $$E[\bar Y \mid \alpha] = \alpha E[Y(1) \mid \alpha] + (1 - \alpha)E[Y(0) \mid \alpha]$$.

**Step 2.** Subtract $$E[Y(0) \mid 0]$$ from each term (weights sum to 1). $$\blacksquare$$

</details>

### Exercise 8 · The direct effect in a linear-in-means model

Let $$Y_i = \mu + \tau D_i + \gamma\frac{k_{-i}}{m - 1} + \varepsilon_i$$, where $$k_{-i}$$ counts treated others in a village of size $$m$$ with exactly $$k = \alpha m$$ treated. Show $$DE(\alpha) = \tau - \gamma/(m - 1)$$, $$IE(\alpha) = \gamma k/(m - 1)$$.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** A treated unit has $$k - 1$$ treated others; an untreated unit has $$k$$.

**Step 2.** $$DE = \tau + \gamma(k - 1)/(m - 1) - \gamma k/(m - 1) = \tau - \gamma/(m - 1)$$.

**Step 3.** Pure controls have no treated others, so $$IE = \gamma k/(m - 1)$$. $$\blacksquare$$ The direct effect is slightly below $$\tau$$ because being treated means one fewer treated neighbour.

</details>

### Exercise 9 · Within-village differences identify DE

Under two-stage randomisation, show the within-saturation difference in means is unbiased for $$DE(\alpha)$$.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Within villages of saturation $$\alpha$$, individuals are randomised, so $$E[Y \mid D = 1, \alpha] = E[Y(1) \mid \alpha]$$, where $$Y(1)$$ is averaged over the random assignment of others.

**Step 2.** Similarly for $$D = 0$$; subtract. $$\blacksquare$$

</details>

### Exercise 10 · Horvitz–Thompson under exposure mappings

Show $$E\big[\mathbf 1\{E_i = e\}Y_i/\pi_i(e)\big] = Y_i(e)$$ when $$Y_i$$ depends on $$\mathbf d$$ only through $$E_i$$ and $$\pi_i(e) > 0$$.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** When $$E_i = e$$, $$Y_i = Y_i(e)$$, a fixed number (randomisation-based inference).

**Step 2.** $$E[\mathbf 1\{E_i = e\}]Y_i(e)/\pi_i(e) = \pi_i(e)Y_i(e)/\pi_i(e)$$. $$\blacksquare$$ Averaging over $$i$$ gives unbiasedness for $$\frac1n\sum Y_i(e)$$.

</details>

## Part 3 · Applied

### Exercise 11 · Deworming with spillovers

A simulation mimics a deworming programme in 600 villages of 8 households: villages are randomised to 0%, 25% or 75% saturation. Deworming has a direct effect $$\tau = 1$$ on a child health index and a spillover of $$2\times$$ (share of other households treated). Estimate the four effects.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** At 25%: $$DE = 0.77$$, $$IE = 0.58$$, $$TE = 1.34$$, $$OE = 0.77$$ (truth 0.71, 0.57, 1.29, 0.75).

**Step 2.** At 75%: $$DE = 0.70$$, $$IE = 1.74$$, $$TE = 2.44$$, $$OE = 2.26$$ (truth 0.71, 1.71, 2.43, 2.25).

**Step 3.** A pooled treated-versus-untreated comparison gives 1.72, which matches none of these.

**Answer:** the policy effect of deworming 75% of households (2.26) is three times the direct effect (0.70): ignoring spillovers would badly understate the programme's value.

</details>

### Exercise 12 · Cash transfers and local prices

Cash transfers to some households in a village may raise local prices, hurting non-recipients. Which estimand captures this, and what design detects it?

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** The indirect effect on untreated households, $$IE(\alpha)$$, which may be negative.

**Step 2.** A saturation design with pure control villages (as in Egger et al.'s Kenya study) identifies it.

</details>

### Exercise 13 · Vaccine trials

Why do individually randomised vaccine trials in a single community understate the population benefit of mass vaccination?

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Controls in the trial benefit from reduced transmission due to vaccinated participants, so the trial measures the direct effect at the trial's coverage.

**Step 2.** Mass vaccination adds indirect protection for everyone; cluster-randomised trials with different coverage levels measure it.

</details>

### Exercise 14 · Peer effects in a smoking-prevention programme

A school programme trains selected students as peer leaders. How would you define exposures for a network analysis?

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Exposure categories: own training (yes/no) × number of trained friends (0, 1, 2+).

**Step 2.** Compute $$\pi_i(e)$$ by simulating the randomisation many times and use Horvitz–Thompson or Hájek estimators.

</details>

### Exercise 15 · Advising a ministry

Explain in two sentences why the deworming programme's value should be judged by the overall effect.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** "Deworming some children reduces worm transmission to their neighbours, so untreated children also benefit."

**Step 2.** "Comparing treated and untreated children in the same village misses this; the overall effect, comparing villages with and without the programme, measures what the ministry actually buys."

</details>

## Verify in Python

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(97)
G, m, tau, gam = 600, 8, 1.0, 2.0                       # villages, households per village, direct, spillover
sat = rng.choice([0.0, .25, .75], size=G)               # stage 1: saturation per village
D = np.concatenate([rng.permutation(np.r_[np.ones(int(a * m)), np.zeros(m - int(a * m))]) for a in sat])
vid = np.repeat(np.arange(G), m); s = np.repeat(sat, m)
k = np.bincount(vid, weights=D)[vid]                    # treated in own village
Y = 5 + tau * D + gam * (k - D) / (m - 1) + np.repeat(.5 * rng.normal(size=G), m) + rng.normal(size=G * m)

print("pooled treated vs untreated:", round(float(Y[D == 1].mean() - Y[D == 0].mean()), 3))
y0 = Y[s == 0].mean()
for a in (.25, .75):
    S, kk = s == a, a * m
    est = [Y[S & (D == 1)].mean() - Y[S & (D == 0)].mean(), Y[S & (D == 0)].mean() - y0,
           Y[S & (D == 1)].mean() - y0, Y[S].mean() - y0]
    tru = [tau - gam / (m - 1), gam * kk / (m - 1), tau + gam * (kk - 1) / (m - 1),
           a * (tau + gam * (kk - 1) / (m - 1)) + (1 - a) * gam * kk / (m - 1)]
    print(f"alpha={a}: DE, IE, TE, OE estimated {np.round(est, 2)}  truth {np.round(tru, 2)}")
print("Ex 3 OE:", round(.75 * 7.43 + .25 * 6.71 - 5, 2))
```

## Key takeaways

- Interference breaks SUTVA; with partial interference, effects are defined at a treatment saturation.
- Direct + indirect = total; the overall effect is the policy-relevant comparison of villages.
- Two-stage randomised saturation designs with pure controls identify all four; analyse at the cluster level, and use exposure mappings for networks.
