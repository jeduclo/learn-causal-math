## Theory

### When back-door fails

Suppose $$X \leftarrow U \to Y$$ with $$U$$ unobserved. No back-door set exists. But if $$X$$ affects $$Y$$ **only** through a mediator $$M$$ that is not itself confounded with $$X$$, we can still identify the effect:

$$
U \to X, \quad U \to Y, \quad X \to M \to Y.
$$

Classic example: smoking $$X$$, tar deposits $$M$$, lung cancer $$Y$$, unobserved genotype $$U$$.

### The criterion

A set $$\mathbf M$$ satisfies the **front-door criterion** relative to $$(X, Y)$$ if

1. $$\mathbf M$$ intercepts **all directed paths** from $$X$$ to $$Y$$;
2. there is **no unblocked back-door path** from $$X$$ to $$\mathbf M$$;
3. **all back-door paths** from $$\mathbf M$$ to $$Y$$ are blocked by $$X$$.

### The formula

If $$\mathbf M$$ satisfies the criterion and $$P(x, \mathbf m) > 0$$,

$$
P(y \mid \operatorname{do}(x)) = \sum_{\mathbf m} P(\mathbf m \mid x)\sum_{x'} P(y \mid \mathbf m, x')\,P(x').
$$

### The logic in two steps

1. **Effect of $$X$$ on $$M$$.** By condition 2, $$P(m \mid \operatorname{do}(x)) = P(m \mid x)$$.
2. **Effect of $$M$$ on $$Y$$.** By condition 3, $$X$$ is a back-door set for $$M \to Y$$: $$P(y \mid \operatorname{do}(m)) = \sum_{x'} P(y \mid m, x')P(x')$$.
3. **Chain them.** By condition 1, $$X$$ affects $$Y$$ only via $$M$$: $$P(y \mid \operatorname{do}(x)) = \sum_m P(m \mid \operatorname{do}(x))\,P(y \mid \operatorname{do}(m))$$.

### The linear case

With $$M = aX + e_M$$ and $$Y = bM + cU + e_Y$$, $$X$$ correlated with $$U$$: estimate $$a$$ by regressing $$M$$ on $$X$$, and $$b$$ by regressing $$Y$$ on $$M$$ **and** $$X$$. The effect is $$\hat a\hat b$$.

### Plausibility

The conditions are strong: no direct effect of $$X$$ on $$Y$$ outside $$M$$, and no unobserved common cause of $$M$$ and $$Y$$. They hold most credibly when $$M$$ is a mechanical step: signing up versus attending, being offered versus receiving, prescribed versus dispensed.

### Exercise 1 · Check the conditions

For each graph, does $$\{M\}$$ satisfy the front-door criterion for $$X \to Y$$? (a) $$U \to X$$, $$U \to Y$$, $$X \to M \to Y$$. (b) Add $$X \to Y$$. (c) Instead add $$U \to M$$. (d) Instead add $$V \to M$$, $$V \to Y$$ with $$V$$ unobserved.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** (a) $$M$$ intercepts $$X \to M \to Y$$; back-door paths from $$X$$ to $$M$$: $$X \leftarrow U \to Y \leftarrow M$$, blocked at the collider $$Y$$; back-door from $$M$$ to $$Y$$: $$M \leftarrow X \leftarrow U \to Y$$, blocked by $$X$$. Satisfied.

**Step 2.** (b) $$X \to Y$$ is a directed path not through $$M$$: condition 1 fails.

**Step 3.** (c) $$X \leftarrow U \to M$$ is an open back-door path from $$X$$ to $$M$$: condition 2 fails.

**Step 4.** (d) $$M \leftarrow V \to Y$$ is a back-door path from $$M$$ to $$Y$$ not blocked by $$X$$: condition 3 fails.

</details>

### Exercise 2 · Linear front door

Regressing $$M$$ on $$X$$ gives 0.5; regressing $$Y$$ on $$M$$ and $$X$$ gives 2.0 on $$M$$. The front-door conditions hold. What is the effect of a one-unit increase in $$X$$?

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Effect of $$X$$ on $$M$$: 0.5. Effect of $$M$$ on $$Y$$: 2.0.

**Answer:** $$0.5 \times 2.0 = 1.0$$.

</details>

### Exercise 3 · The first step

Why is $$P(m \mid \operatorname{do}(x)) = P(m \mid x)$$ in graph (a) of Exercise 1?

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** The only back-door path from $$X$$ to $$M$$ is $$X \leftarrow U \to Y \leftarrow M$$.

**Step 2.** It has a collider at $$Y$$, so it is blocked by the empty set.

**Answer:** $$\varnothing$$ satisfies the back-door criterion for $$X \to M$$, so conditioning equals intervening.

</details>

### Exercise 4 · A small front-door computation

$$P(X = 1) = 0.4$$. $$P(M = 1 \mid X = 1) = 0.8$$, $$P(M = 1 \mid X = 0) = 0.3$$. $$P(Y = 1 \mid M, X)$$: $$(M, X) = (1, 1)$$: 0.6; $$(0, 1)$$: 0.4; $$(1, 0)$$: 0.5; $$(0, 0)$$: 0.3. Compute $$P(Y = 1 \mid \operatorname{do}(X = 1))$$ by the front-door formula.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Inner sums: $$m = 1$$: $$0.4(0.6) + 0.6(0.5) = 0.24 + 0.30 = 0.54$$; $$m = 0$$: $$0.4(0.4) + 0.6(0.3) = 0.16 + 0.18 = 0.34$$.

**Step 2.** Outer sum with $$P(m \mid X = 1)$$: $$0.8(0.54) + 0.2(0.34) = 0.432 + 0.068 = 0.50$$.

**Answer:** 0.50.

</details>

### Exercise 5 · Why average over $$x'$$?

In the second step, why do we average $$P(y \mid m, x')$$ over $$P(x')$$ instead of using $$P(y \mid m, x)$$ at the treatment value $$x$$?

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** We need the effect of $$M$$ on $$Y$$ in the *whole population*, $$P(y \mid \operatorname{do}(m))$$.

**Step 2.** $$X$$ is used only as a back-door adjustment variable for $$M \to Y$$ (it blocks $$M \leftarrow X \leftarrow U \to Y$$), so it must be averaged over its marginal distribution, as in any back-door formula.

**Answer:** using $$P(y \mid m, x)$$ alone would describe the $$U$$-mix of people with $$X = x$$, which is confounded.

</details>

## Part 2 · Proofs

### Exercise 6 · Step 1 in general

Prove $$P(m \mid \operatorname{do}(x)) = P(m \mid x)$$ under condition 2.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Condition 2 says $$\varnothing$$ blocks every back-door path from $$X$$ to $$M$$, and $$\varnothing$$ contains no descendants of $$X$$.

**Step 2.** So $$\varnothing$$ satisfies the back-door criterion for $$(X, M)$$, and the adjustment formula reduces to $$P(m \mid \operatorname{do}(x)) = P(m \mid x)$$. $$\blacksquare$$

</details>

### Exercise 7 · Step 2 in general

Prove $$P(y \mid \operatorname{do}(m)) = \sum_{x} P(y \mid m, x)P(x)$$ under the front-door conditions.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Condition 3: $$\{X\}$$ blocks all back-door paths from $$M$$ to $$Y$$.

**Step 2.** $$X$$ is not a descendant of $$M$$: $$M$$ lies on the directed paths from $$X$$, and acyclicity forbids $$M \to \cdots \to X$$.

**Step 3.** So $$\{X\}$$ satisfies the back-door criterion for $$(M, Y)$$ and the adjustment formula gives the result. $$\blacksquare$$

</details>

### Exercise 8 · Chaining the steps

Using the SCM $$U \to X$$, $$U \to Y$$, $$X \to M \to Y$$, prove $$P(y \mid \operatorname{do}(x)) = \sum_m P(m \mid \operatorname{do}(x))P(y \mid \operatorname{do}(m))$$.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Truncated factorisation for $$\operatorname{do}(x)$$: $$P(u, m, y \mid \operatorname{do}(x)) = P(u)P(m \mid x)P(y \mid m, u)$$.

**Step 2.** Sum over $$u$$ and $$m$$: $$P(y \mid \operatorname{do}(x)) = \sum_m P(m \mid x)\sum_u P(y \mid m, u)P(u)$$.

**Step 3.** Truncated factorisation for $$\operatorname{do}(m)$$: $$P(u, x, y \mid \operatorname{do}(m)) = P(u)P(x \mid u)P(y \mid m, u)$$; summing over $$u, x$$ gives $$P(y \mid \operatorname{do}(m)) = \sum_u P(y \mid m, u)P(u)$$.

**Step 4.** By Exercise 6, $$P(m \mid x) = P(m \mid \operatorname{do}(x))$$. Substitute both into Step 2. $$\blacksquare$$ Combined with Exercise 7, this proves the front-door formula.

</details>

### Exercise 9 · The linear product

With $$X = gU + e_X$$, $$M = aX + e_M$$, $$Y = bM + cU + e_Y$$ (all disturbances independent), prove the population coefficient on $$M$$ in the regression of $$Y$$ on $$(M, X)$$ is $$b$$, so $$\hat a\hat b \to ab$$.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** $$E[Y \mid M, X] = bM + cE[U \mid M, X]$$.

**Step 2.** $$M = aX + e_M$$ with $$e_M$$ independent of $$(U, X)$$; so given $$X$$, $$M$$ carries no information about $$U$$: $$E[U \mid M, X] = E[U \mid X]$$, a linear function of $$X$$ only.

**Step 3.** So $$E[Y \mid M, X] = bM + (\text{function of } X)$$: the coefficient on $$M$$ is $$b$$.

**Step 4.** The regression of $$M$$ on $$X$$ recovers $$a$$ because $$e_M$$ is independent of $$X$$. $$\blacksquare$$

</details>

### Exercise 10 · A direct effect breaks it

Add a direct effect, $$Y = bM + dX + cU + e_Y$$. What does $$\hat a\hat b$$ converge to, and what is the true total effect?

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** The argument of Exercise 9 still gives coefficient $$b$$ on $$M$$ (the $$dX$$ term is a function of $$X$$).

**Step 2.** So $$\hat a\hat b \to ab$$.

**Step 3.** True total effect: $$ab + d$$.

**Answer:** the front-door estimate misses the direct effect entirely; condition 1 is essential. $$\blacksquare$$

</details>

## Part 3 · Applied

### Exercise 11 · Smoking, tar and cancer

Observational data: $$P(X = 1) = 0.5$$ smokers; $$P(M = 1 \mid X = 1) = 0.9$$ and $$P(M = 1 \mid X = 0) = 0.1$$ heavy tar deposits; cancer rates $$P(Y = 1 \mid M, X)$$: $$(1, 1)$$: 0.44; $$(0, 1)$$: 0.34; $$(1, 0)$$: 0.26; $$(0, 0)$$: 0.16. Compute the naive risk difference and the front-door effect.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Naive: $$P(Y = 1 \mid X = 1) = 0.9(0.44) + 0.1(0.34) = 0.43$$ and $$P(Y = 1 \mid X = 0) = 0.1(0.26) + 0.9(0.16) = 0.17$$; difference 0.26.

**Step 2.** Effect of tar, averaging over smoking: $$P(Y \mid \operatorname{do}(M = 1)) = 0.5(0.44) + 0.5(0.26) = 0.35$$; $$P(Y \mid \operatorname{do}(M = 0)) = 0.5(0.34) + 0.5(0.16) = 0.25$$.

**Step 3.** $$P(Y \mid \operatorname{do}(X = 1)) = 0.9(0.35) + 0.1(0.25) = 0.34$$.

**Step 4.** $$P(Y \mid \operatorname{do}(X = 0)) = 0.1(0.35) + 0.9(0.25) = 0.26$$.

**Answer:** causal risk difference 0.08, versus a naive 0.26. (These numbers come from an SCM with a genotype that raises both smoking and cancer; the code checks that 0.08 is the true effect.)

</details>

### Exercise 12 · Signing up versus showing up

A job-training programme records who **signed up** ($$X$$), who **attended** ($$M$$) and later earnings ($$Y$$). Motivation is unobserved. Discuss each front-door condition for the effect of signing up.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Condition 1: signing up should affect earnings only through attending. Plausible, unless signing up itself changes behaviour (e.g. it triggers other services or job-search requirements).

**Step 2.** Condition 2: no unblocked confounding between signing up and attending. Questionable: motivated people both sign up and attend.

**Step 3.** Condition 3: given sign-up status, attendance is unconfounded with earnings. Questionable for the same reason.

**Answer:** among sign-ups, attendance is nearly mechanical (most attend), which makes conditions 2–3 more credible; studies of this design find estimates close to experimental benchmarks for some groups and not others. Plausibility must be argued, not assumed.

</details>

### Exercise 13 · Mobile money and savings

A mobile-money app ($$X$$, adopted or not) can raise household savings $$Y$$ only through account usage $$M$$ (deposits per month). Adoption and savings share unobserved financial literacy. Regressions: usage on adoption gives 0.6 deposits; savings on usage and adoption gives $50 per deposit on usage. Estimate the effect of adoption on savings.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Effect of adoption on usage: $$\hat a = 0.6$$.

**Step 2.** Effect of usage on savings, adjusting for adoption: $$\hat b = 50$$.

**Answer:** $$0.6 \times 50 = 30$$ dollars per household. The naive regression of savings on adoption would also pick up literacy.

</details>

### Exercise 14 · What if the app has a direct effect?

Suppose the app also raises savings by $10 directly (through reminders that work even without deposits). What does the front-door estimate from Exercise 13 converge to, and what is the true effect?

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** By Exercise 10, $$\hat a\hat b \to ab = 30$$.

**Step 2.** True total effect: $$30 + 10 = 40$$.

**Answer:** the front-door estimate understates the effect by $10 (25%). In the simulation below, the front-door estimate is about 30 and the truth 40.

</details>

### Exercise 15 · Nurse staffing and mortality

A hospital network asks whether higher nurse staffing ($$X$$) lowers in-hospital mortality ($$Y$$). Wards with sicker patients get more nurses, and severity is poorly measured. The analyst proposes using monitoring frequency ($$M$$, vital-sign checks per day) as a front-door variable. List what must be true and one likely failure for each condition.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Condition 1: staffing affects mortality only through monitoring. Likely failure: nurses also deliver medication on time and prevent falls; those channels bypass $$M$$.

**Step 2.** Condition 2: nothing unmeasured affects both staffing and monitoring. Likely failure: severity raises staffing and monitoring protocols directly.

**Step 3.** Condition 3: given staffing, monitoring is unconfounded with mortality. Likely failure: sicker patients are monitored more and die more.

**Answer:** monitoring fails all three; the front door is not credible here. A better design might use staffing shocks (nurse absences, Phase 5 instruments).

</details>

## Verify in Python

```python
import itertools
import numpy as np

# Exercise 11: the SCM behind the table (U = genotype, unobserved)
pU = .5; pX = {1: .8, 0: .2}; pM = {1: .9, 0: .1}; pY = {(0, 0): .1, (1, 0): .2, (0, 1): .4, (1, 1): .5}   # key (m, u)
truth = {x: sum((pM[x] if m else 1 - pM[x]) * sum((pU if u else 1 - pU) * pY[m, u] for u in (0, 1)) for m in (0, 1)) for x in (0, 1)}
J = {(u, x, m): (pU if u else 1 - pU) * (pX[u] if x else 1 - pX[u]) * (pM[x] if m else 1 - pM[x])
     for u, x, m in itertools.product((0, 1), repeat=3)}
Px = {x: sum(v for k, v in J.items() if k[1] == x) for x in (0, 1)}
Pm_x = {(m, x): sum(v for k, v in J.items() if k[1] == x and k[2] == m) / Px[x] for m in (0, 1) for x in (0, 1)}
Py_mx = {(m, x): sum(v * pY[m, k[0]] for k, v in J.items() if k[1] == x and k[2] == m) / (Px[x] * Pm_x[m, x]) for m in (0, 1) for x in (0, 1)}
print("observed P(Y=1|M,X):", {k: round(v, 3) for k, v in Py_mx.items()})
naive = {x: sum(Pm_x[m, x] * Py_mx[m, x] for m in (0, 1)) for x in (0, 1)}
front = {x: sum(Pm_x[m, x] * sum(Py_mx[m, xp] * Px[xp] for xp in (0, 1)) for m in (0, 1)) for x in (0, 1)}
print(f"naive {naive[1] - naive[0]:.3f}  front-door {front[1] - front[0]:.3f}  truth {truth[1] - truth[0]:.3f}")

# Exercises 13-14: linear front door with and without a direct effect
rng = np.random.default_rng(54); n = 500_000
L = rng.normal(size=n)                                 # financial literacy (unobserved)
X = (L + rng.normal(size=n) > 0).astype(float)
M = 0.6 * X + rng.normal(size=n)
for d in (0, 10):
    Y = 50 * M + d * X + 40 * L + rng.normal(0, 20, n)
    a = np.polyfit(X, M, 1)[0]
    b = np.linalg.lstsq(np.column_stack([np.ones(n), M, X]), Y, rcond=None)[0][1]
    naive = Y[X == 1].mean() - Y[X == 0].mean()
    print(f"direct effect {d}: naive {naive:.1f}, front-door {a * b:.1f}, truth {30 + d}")
```

## Key takeaways

- The front door identifies an effect despite unobserved treatment–outcome confounding, if a mediator captures the whole effect, is unconfounded with the treatment, and is unconfounded with the outcome given the treatment.
- Formula: $$P(y \mid \operatorname{do}(x)) = \sum_m P(m \mid x)\sum_{x'} P(y \mid m, x')P(x')$$; in linear models, the product of two regression coefficients.
- Its assumptions are strong; they are most credible when the mediator is a mechanical step of the treatment.
