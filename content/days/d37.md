## Theory

### The sharp null

Fisher (1935) tested the **sharp null**

$$
H_0: Y_i(1) = Y_i(0) \quad\text{for every unit } i.
$$

Under $$H_0$$, each unit's outcome would have been the same whatever its assignment, so the observed $$Y_i$$ fills in *both* potential outcomes. The whole science table is known.

### The randomisation distribution

Pick a test statistic $$S(\mathbf T, \mathbf Y)$$, e.g. the difference in means. Because the science table is known under $$H_0$$, we can compute $$S$$ for *every* assignment the design could have produced. The exact p-value is

$$
p = \frac{\#\{\text{assignments } \mathbf t: |S(\mathbf t, \mathbf Y)| \ge |S^{obs}|\}}{\#\{\text{assignments}\}}.
$$

No normality, no large sample, no model: the only randomness is the one the researcher created. When there are too many assignments to list, draw $$R$$ at random (Monte Carlo); the p-value then has standard error $$\sqrt{p(1 - p)/R}$$.

### Choices and extensions

- **Statistic:** any function of the data. Rank statistics (Wilcoxon) resist outliers.
- **Other sharp nulls:** $$H_0: Y_i(1) = Y_i(0) + \tau_0$$ for all $$i$$. Subtract $$\tau_0$$ from treated outcomes and test again. Collecting all $$\tau_0$$ not rejected gives a **confidence interval** (test inversion).
- **Design:** permute exactly as the design randomised: within blocks for blocked designs, whole clusters for cluster designs.
- **Sharp versus weak null:** the sharp null (no effect for anyone) is stronger than Neyman's weak null (zero *average* effect). Randomisation tests are exact for the former and only approximately valid for the latter.

## Part 1 · Warm-up

### Exercise 1 · How many assignments?

A pilot randomises 4 of 8 villages to a microfinance programme. How many assignments are possible, and what is the smallest achievable two-sided p-value?

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** $$\binom84 = 70$$ assignments.

**Step 2.** The observed assignment always counts itself. With a symmetric two-sided statistic, its mirror image (treated and control swapped) gives the same $$|S|$$, so at least 2 of 70 qualify.

**Answer:** 70 assignments; smallest two-sided p-value $$2/70 \approx 0.029$$.

</details>

### Exercise 2 · Imputing under the sharp null

A treated village has profit 12 and a control village 9. Under $$H_0$$, what are their missing potential outcomes?

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** No effect for anyone: $$Y(0) = Y(1)$$ for each village.

**Step 2.** Treated village: $$Y(0) = 12$$. Control village: $$Y(1) = 9$$.

</details>

### Exercise 3 · The statistic under another assignment

Profits are 12, 15, 11, 14 (treated villages A–D) and 9, 10, 13, 8 (controls E–H). Under $$H_0$$, what difference in means would the assignment {A, B, E, F} have produced?

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Under $$H_0$$ outcomes do not move: "treated" A, B, E, F have $$(12 + 15 + 9 + 10)/4 = 11.5$$.

**Step 2.** "Controls" C, D, G, H have $$(11 + 14 + 13 + 8)/4 = 11.5$$.

**Answer:** 0.

</details>

### Exercise 4 · From counts to a p-value

Of the 70 assignments, 8 give $$|\bar Y_1 - \bar Y_0| \ge 3$$ (the observed value). What is the p-value?

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** $$p = 8/70 \approx 0.114$$.

**Answer:** 0.11: not significant at 5% or 10%.

</details>

### Exercise 5 · Monte Carlo precision

With 70 million possible assignments you sample $$R = 1{,}000$$. If the true p-value is 0.05, what is the Monte Carlo SE? How many draws give an SE of 0.002?

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** SE $$= \sqrt{0.05 \times 0.95/1000} \approx 0.0069$$.

**Step 2.** Solve $$\sqrt{0.0475/R} = 0.002$$: $$R = 0.0475/0.000004 \approx 11{,}875$$.

**Answer:** SE ≈ 0.007 with 1,000 draws; about 12,000 draws for SE 0.002.

</details>

## Part 2 · Proofs

### Exercise 6 · The test is exact

Under the sharp null and the actual design, prove that $$P(p \le \alpha) \le \alpha$$.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Under $$H_0$$ the outcome vector $$\mathbf Y$$ is fixed whatever the assignment, so the statistic $$S(\mathbf t)$$ is a fixed number for each of the $$K$$ possible assignments.

**Step 2.** The observed assignment is one of them, chosen uniformly at random by the design. So $$|S^{obs}|$$ is a uniformly random draw from the $$K$$ values $$|S(\mathbf t)|$$.

**Step 3.** The p-value is the share of values at least as large as the draw. A uniformly random draw lands in the top $$100\alpha\%$$ with probability at most $$\alpha$$ (ties only make the p-value larger).

**Answer:** $$P(p \le \alpha) \le \alpha$$: exact Type I error control in any sample size. $$\blacksquare$$

</details>

### Exercise 7 · Confidence intervals by inversion

For $$H_0(\tau_0): Y_i(1) = Y_i(0) + \tau_0$$, show how to impute the science table, and explain why $$\{\tau_0: p(\tau_0) > \alpha\}$$ is a $$1 - \alpha$$ confidence set.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Treated units: $$Y_i(0) = Y_i - \tau_0$$. Controls: $$Y_i(1) = Y_i + \tau_0$$.

**Step 2.** Equivalently, subtract $$\tau_0$$ from treated outcomes; under $$H_0(\tau_0)$$ the adjusted outcomes satisfy the sharp null of no effect, so Exercise 6 applies.

**Step 3.** If the true effect is $$\tau^*$$, the test of $$H_0(\tau^*)$$ rejects with probability at most $$\alpha$$, so $$\tau^*$$ is in the set with probability at least $$1 - \alpha$$. $$\blacksquare$$

</details>

### Exercise 8 · The null distribution is centred at zero

Under the sharp null and complete randomisation, show the randomisation distribution of the difference in means has mean zero.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Under $$H_0$$, $$Y_i(1) = Y_i(0) = Y_i$$, so the finite-population ATE is 0.

**Step 2.** By Day 31, Exercise 8, the difference in means averages to the ATE over all assignments.

**Answer:** mean 0. $$\blacksquare$$

</details>

### Exercise 9 · Sharp versus weak null

Construct a two-unit example where the weak null (ATE = 0) holds but the sharp null fails.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Unit 1: $$Y(0) = 0$$, $$Y(1) = 5$$ (effect +5). Unit 2: $$Y(0) = 5$$, $$Y(1) = 0$$ (effect −5).

**Step 2.** ATE $$= (5 - 5)/2 = 0$$: the weak null holds.

**Step 3.** Individual effects are not zero: the sharp null fails.

**Answer:** a randomisation test of the sharp null can reject here even though the average effect is zero; it tests "no effect for anyone".

</details>

### Exercise 10 · Rank statistics

Show that, under the sharp null, the randomisation distribution of the Wilcoxon rank-sum statistic (the sum of treated units' ranks) depends only on $$n$$ and $$n_1$$ (with no ties), not on the outcome values.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Under $$H_0$$ the outcomes, hence their ranks $$1, \dots, n$$, are fixed.

**Step 2.** Each assignment picks $$n_1$$ of these ranks; the statistic is their sum.

**Step 3.** The set of possible sums and their frequencies depend only on which subsets of $$\{1, \dots, n\}$$ of size $$n_1$$ exist.

**Answer:** the null distribution can be tabulated once for each $$(n, n_1)$$; an extreme outlier changes one rank by at most a few places, so the test is robust.

</details>

## Part 3 · Applied

### Exercise 11 · A microfinance pilot

Using the eight villages of Exercise 3 (treated 12, 15, 11, 14; control 9, 10, 13, 8), compute the observed difference in means, the exact one-sided and two-sided p-values (see the code), and compare with a t-test.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Treated mean $$= 52/4 = 13$$; control mean $$= 40/4 = 10$$; observed difference 3.

**Step 2.** Enumerate all 70 assignments under $$H_0$$: 4 give a difference $$\ge 3$$ and 8 give $$|\text{difference}| \ge 3$$.

**Step 3.** One-sided $$p = 4/70 \approx 0.057$$; two-sided $$p = 8/70 \approx 0.114$$.

**Step 4.** A Welch t-test gives $$p \approx 0.08$$, somewhat smaller, but it relies on approximate normality with only 8 villages.

**Answer:** no significant evidence at 5%. The randomisation p-value is exact whatever the distribution of profits.

</details>

### Exercise 12 · A small hospital trial

10 patients are randomised, 5 per arm; all 5 treated recover versus 1 of 5 controls. Compute Fisher's exact p-values.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Under $$H_0$$, the 6 recoverers would recover whatever the arm. The number of recoverers landing in the treated arm is hypergeometric: choose 5 treated from 10 patients, 6 of whom recover.

**Step 2.** All 5 treated recover: $$\binom65\binom40/\binom{10}{5} = 6/252 \approx 0.024$$. That is the one-sided p-value (no table is more extreme).

**Step 3.** The opposite extreme (only 1 treated recoverer) also has probability $$\binom61\binom44/252 = 6/252$$.

**Step 4.** Two-sided $$p = 12/252 \approx 0.048$$.

**Answer:** one-sided 0.024, two-sided 0.048: significant at 5% with just 10 patients, and no large-sample approximation needed.

</details>

### Exercise 13 · Testing a specific effect

For the microfinance pilot, test the sharp null that the programme raises *every* village's profit by exactly 2.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** Subtract 2 from treated profits: 10, 13, 9, 12; controls stay 9, 10, 13, 8.

**Step 2.** The adjusted difference is $$11 - 10 = 1$$; the randomisation test of no effect on the adjusted data gives $$p \approx 0.66$$ (code).

**Step 3.** Repeating for every $$\tau_0$$ and keeping those with $$p > 0.10$$ gives a 90% interval; here it spans roughly 0 to 6.

**Answer:** an effect of 2 is entirely consistent with the data; the interval is wide because there are only 8 villages.

</details>

### Exercise 14 · Clustered randomisation inference

A study randomises 10 of 20 schools; outcomes are measured for 3,000 pupils. How should the randomisation test permute the data, and why?

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** The design assigned *schools*, so the null distribution must come from re-assigning whole schools: $$\binom{20}{10} = 184{,}756$$ possibilities.

**Step 2.** Permuting individual pupils would generate assignments the design could never produce (mixed schools) and understate variability, because pupils in a school share shocks.

**Answer:** permute at the school level, using a school-level or pupil-level statistic computed for each re-assignment.

</details>

### Exercise 15 · One treated state

A tax reform is adopted by 1 of 20 states. You compute a statistic for each state as if it had been the one treated. What is the smallest possible p-value, and what does that imply?

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

**Step 1.** If the reform were assigned at random to one state, there are 20 equally likely possibilities.

**Step 2.** The smallest one-sided p-value is $$1/20 = 0.05$$ (the treated state is the most extreme of all 20).

**Answer:** 0.05. With one treated unit, randomisation inference can never be very decisive; this placebo logic underlies inference for synthetic control (Day 86). (Here the "as if random" assumption must be argued, since states choose reforms.)

</details>

## Verify in Python

```python
import itertools
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

y = np.array([12, 15, 11, 14, 9, 10, 13, 8.]); treated = np.array([1, 1, 1, 1, 0, 0, 0, 0], bool)

def rand_test(y, treated, tau0=0.0):
    z = y - tau0 * treated                                   # impute under H0(tau0)
    obs = z[treated].mean() - z[~treated].mean()
    dist = np.array([z[list(c)].mean() - np.delete(z, list(c)).mean()
                     for c in itertools.combinations(range(len(z)), treated.sum())])
    return obs, (dist >= obs - 1e-9).mean(), (np.abs(dist) >= abs(obs) - 1e-9).mean()

obs, p1, p2 = rand_test(y, treated)
print(f"observed {obs}, one-sided p {p1:.3f}, two-sided p {p2:.3f}")
print("Welch t-test p:", round(stats.ttest_ind(y[treated], y[~treated], equal_var=False).pvalue, 3))
print("test of tau0 = 2: p =", round(rand_test(y, treated, 2)[2], 3))
ci = [t for t in np.arange(-4, 10, 0.1) if rand_test(y, treated, t)[2] > 0.10]
print(f"90% interval by inversion: [{min(ci):.1f}, {max(ci):.1f}]")

# Fisher's exact test for the hospital trial
print("Fisher exact (two-sided):", round(stats.fisher_exact([[5, 0], [1, 4]])[1], 4),
      " one-sided:", round(stats.fisher_exact([[5, 0], [1, 4]], alternative="greater")[1], 4))
```

## Key takeaways

- Under the sharp null, every potential outcome is known, so the randomisation itself gives an exact null distribution.
- Permute exactly as the design randomised (within blocks, by cluster).
- Inverting sharp-null tests yields confidence intervals; randomisation tests concern "no effect for anyone".
